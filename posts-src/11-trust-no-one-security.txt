"Trust no one" is the most famous line in The X-Files. It started as advice from an informant and became a whole philosophy. It also became Mulder's computer password, trustno1, which tells you something about the gap between having good instincts and having good security habits.

## The password problem

Mulder's password is a perfect bad password: a well-known phrase, connected to the owner, with a single digit tacked on. In the real world, trustno1 has shown up for years on lists of the most commonly used passwords. If a password is famous enough to appear in a TV show, it's in every attacker's wordlist.

The lesson is the same today: use long, random passphrases or a password manager, and turn on multi-factor authentication. Any visitor who found the sticky note in the basement on this site has just proved the point.

## "Kill Switch" saw the future

Season five's "Kill Switch" was co-written by William Gibson, the novelist who helped invent cyberpunk, along with Tom Maddox. It's about an artificial intelligence that has escaped into the internet and protects itself by any means necessary, including a military satellite. In 1998 it looked like science fiction. Today, a story about software that spreads across networks and fights being shut off doesn't seem quite so far-fetched.

## Surveillance as the default

The show's paranoia was mostly about governments: wiretaps, tracking devices, implants, files kept on ordinary people. Mulder assumes he's being watched, and he's usually right. What the show didn't predict is how much of that surveillance we'd end up carrying around voluntarily. The revival made that point in episodes like "Rm9sbG93ZXJz," where a smart home, a smart car, and a smart restaurant turn on the agents.

## The Lone Gunmen model

The Lone Gunmen are the show's best example of how security people actually work. They verify everyone at the door, they assume their phones are tapped, they keep their own copies of everything, and they share what they find. Paranoid, yes. Also mostly correct.

## The real lesson

"Trust no one" isn't great life advice, but it's not bad engineering advice. Verify things before you rely on them. Assume someone is trying to get in. And don't put your password on a sticky note.
