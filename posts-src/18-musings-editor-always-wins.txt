title: The Editor Always Wins
date: 2026-11-02
tag: Form
hue: 70
summary: Musings of a Cigarette Smoking Man is a story about a writer whose publisher changed his ending. Its own executives then changed its title, and its planned last shot vanished from the cut. The conspiracy, the episode admits, is one man's account of a history that had to include him.
---
The hour opens with the Cigarette Smoking Man setting up a listening post in an empty building, pointed at the Lone Gunmen's office across the street. Inside, Frohike is telling Mulder and Scully what he has found out about him. The man listens to his own biography with a rifle beside him and a pack of cigarettes within reach, and the biography is the next forty minutes. Glen Morgan wrote it, James Wong directed it, his first episode as a director, and it aired on November 17, 1996.

Every layer of the hour is somebody's draft. Frohike's story, which he admits at the end came out of a magazine he subscribes to. The magazine, *Roman a Clef*, which bought a pulp adventure from a writer calling himself Raul Bloodworth and changed his ending. And the production, in which the writer's own title and final shot were taken away by his executives. The man who believes he runs history cannot get his last page past an editor, and neither could the people who wrote him.

## A nobody who was there for everything

The structure is titled parts, the first headed "Things really did go well in Dealey Plaza," the last set in March 1992 with the X-Files themselves. Mulder is a couple of lines heard through a wall; Scully appears only in a clip from the pilot. The hour belongs to Chris Owens, as the young Army captain who does not smoke and would rather read a bad novel than sit through a good movie, and to William B. Davis, who takes over once the character has become the man we know.

Owens is recruited to shoot the president, runs Oswald as the patsy, and lights his first cigarette. Meghan Deans, at Reactor, caught how much of the Dallas sequence is spent watching the wrong man get taken down: the episode gives Oswald's arrest the screen time a thriller would give the killer's escape. The famous name is a decoy and the author stays out of frame. Then Memphis. He hears King speak about poverty and Vietnam, tells his colleagues he respects the man and that he is now talking like a Maoist, and takes the job himself. Morgan, at a PhileFest panel [written up by a fan site](https://www.eatthecorn.com/2024/07/05/philefest-the-wongs/), said the Memphis sequence had a magnificent black and white crane shot that Wong had to cut in half. Wong, whose only directing before this was second unit on *Space: Above and Beyond*, has said he was more prepared for this episode than for anything before or after, down to the shot lists. The Television Academy nominated him the following summer, the series' only directing nomination before Chris Carter's in 1998.

The placement of one man at both assassinations has the look of *Forrest Gump*, which had been out two years, and the episode knows it: the newsstand monologue near the end is the box-of-chocolates line turned sour, and Zack Handlen at The A.V. Club read the reference as more than a parody. Gump was the decade's fantasy of a nobody who happened to be there for everything. Here a somebody needs you to believe he was there for everything, and the one eyewitness willing to say so is a man who reads too many magazines.

## The failed writer

The Smoking Man writes. In the 1960s scenes he works on a manuscript between assignments; later, under a pseudonym, he sends a pulp novel out to publishers. The magazine that buys it is, in Handlen's description, an especially sleazy *Hustler*-style thing, and it alters the ending. The Smoking Man sits on the street beside a homeless man who has fished a box of chocolates out of the trash, and tells him life is like that box, "a cheap, thoughtless, perfunctory gift" nobody asked for. The Christmas Eve 1991 staff meeting has Anita Hill and the Rodney King trial on its agenda, puts Saddam Hussein on hold on line two, and ends with the announcement that what he does not want to see is the Bills winning the Super Bowl. Are those Frohike's embellishments, or the Smoking Man's own, or the magazine's? The episode will not say.

Glen Morgan, in a 2024 podcast interview [summarized at the TenThirteen interview database](https://eatthecorn.com/?p=4758), said he wrote it as though the Smoking Man had done all of it, with a Lex Luthor comic as his model, and that over the years he came to credit Carter with the better reading: the man is a failed writer who keeps reinforcing his own memory until he believes he did these things. Darin Morgan's *The Lost Art of Forehead Sweat*, two decades later, plays the same idea as farce. On set, by Glen Morgan's account in the same interview, Davis gave Wong grief because he thought the script was making fun of him. Davis [said in 2018](https://www.showbizjunkies.com/tv/x-files-cigarette-smoking-man-interview/) that the episode was "not very accurate" about the character's real backstory. He may be right about the facts and wrong about the mockery. The performance works because he plays the pulp as truth, with the same stillness he brought to the Syndicate's conference table, and a man who needs the story to be true would play it that way.

Owens, for his part, studied tape of Davis smoking until, as a 2002 profile [put it](https://www.eatthecorn.com/itw/2002/05/01/tv-now-com-2/), he had "the finger role down just right." A younger self is built to match the older self's image of himself, hands first. There is a further joke in the casting: Owens came back the next season as Jeffrey Spender, the Smoking Man's son, so the actor who played the father's fiction of himself went on to play a son the father could not script.

## The series as one of his drafts

In March 1992 the Smoking Man listens through a bugged briefcase to Mulder and Scully's first exchange, and Scully's appearance in the hour is a clip lifted from the pilot, the episode in which Davis stood in a corner of an FBI office and said nothing. The series inserts its own first episode into his story as an event he attended, which means the show you are watching is being claimed as a scene in his memoir. Deans notices that by the last chapter he has turned the surveillance off and the framing device, as she puts it, has lost a side: he was remembering, not listening. He has heard enough of his biography.

## The ending nobody got

Then the production story, which rhymes with the plot. The script was classified "Top Secret," Davis [told Cox News Service](https://www.squidge.org/nroberts/csmarticles.html) before it aired, and the actors' agents got scenes rather than the whole script. Morgan's working title was "Memoirs of a Cigarette Smoking Man." Carter and the executive Ken Horton, Morgan says, wanted "Musings" because it was more ambiguous, and they did not want Frohike to die. Morgan and Wong shot his death anyway, with a sign and Hershey's syrup, and tried to keep it in the cut the network would see. The shot, Morgan says, disappeared. Frank Spotnitz, according to a fan's notes on [another PhileFest panel](https://www.eatthecorn.com/2024/07/28/philefest-creatives-panel/), lobbied Carter against the killing. Before the episode aired Spotnitz had told the same Cox reporter, "We will never find out his real name." What aired is a rifle sight on Frohike's back, and a line, "I can kill you whenever I please. But not today."

So the hour about a writer whose editor rewrote his ending had its ending rewritten, and a memoir became musings. Whatever that says about Morgan and Wong, it says more about where the mythology stood in 1996. Carter needed the conspiracy to stay a story that could be denied, the same need the pulp magazine had for a happier last page. By refusing to let its villain write his own ending, the series showed what kind of document the conspiracy was: an account, by an interested party, of a history that had to include him.

Davis took the title for his memoir, *Where There's Smoke: Musings of a Cigarette Smoking Man*, published by ECW Press in October 2011. The actor who felt mocked by the script signed its name to his life. Deans put the hour in one sentence: "Anytime a man tells a story, that's just the man's story."
