title: Every Lie Gets the Same Lighting
date: 2026-10-19
tag: Form
hue: 90
summary: In Jose Chung's From Outer Space, Rob Bowman shoots a power-line worker's screenplay with the same conviction as a hypnotized teenager's memory, and by the end the two agents are evidence in somebody else's file.
---
The camera drifts past a dark, angular mass that fills the frame like a spaceship. It is the underside of a utility crane, and a man in a bucket is working the power lines above a country road at night. So begins *Jose Chung's From Outer Space*, written by Darin Morgan, directed by Rob Bowman, broadcast on April 12, 1996. Emily St. James, at The A.V. Club, saw a Star Destroyer in the shape before it resolved, and called the shot the intellectual counterpart of Chung's closing monologue. The first image in the hour is a thing that looks like evidence and is only equipment.

The episode has been admired for thirty years as a script, and most of the praise goes to the dialogue and the nested structure. The director deserves a share. Every version of the night in question, from a sober Air Force cover story to a power-line worker's screenplay about a creature from the earth's core, is photographed, lit, scored and performed with the same conviction. The viewer gets five incompatible versions of one night and no visual cue to rank them by. Television usually flags the lie with a different stock, a softer lens or a wink from the actor. Morgan told Cinefantastique that Bowman had to read the script fifteen times before he understood it, and the understanding shows in what Bowman declined to do. The hour will not editorialize about whatever its camera is pointed at.

## Chrissy and Harold

Below the crane, two teenagers, Chrissy and Harold, are pulled from their car by gray aliens, and a larger creature then attacks the aliens, one of whom asks the other what that thing is. The scene has the show's usual night exterior and its usual dread, and the aliens, it turns out, are witnesses like everyone else.

## Five versions, one lighting plan

Morgan told Cinefantastique he had first planned for Chung, the novelist researching a book on the case, to interview a different person in each act, and dropped the idea as too complicated. Scully became the main narrator, with Chung talking to the UFO enthusiast Blaine in the third act. The result is a set of stories nested inside one story. In Morgan's words, "even when Scully is telling her story, she's actually telling everyone else's account." She relays Chrissy's hypnotic regression, Harold's, Roky's, the detective's and Mulder's, and she edits as she goes. When Detective Manners swears, Scully substitutes a "bleep," so what we hear is her censorship. The evidence has been cleaned at the source, by the narrator the show has always trusted most.

Chrissy's first hypnosis session produces a spaceship, gray examiners, and a girl told it is all for the good of the planet. Her second produces Air Force men and the CIA, wiping her memory for the good of the country. Bowman stages the second as a recasting of the first: Chrissy on a glass table among aliens becomes Chrissy in a chair in a bright room, with the Air Force and CIA men standing where the aliens stood. Morgan preferred altered memories to erased ones; someone changing what you remember frightened him more than abduction did. The direction honors that preference by shooting neither version as the dream.

Roky Crikenson's version is where most directors would have cheated. He saw the abduction from his crane, was visited by two men in black who assured him he had seen Venus, and wrote it all up as a screenplay, with the creature from the teaser given a name, Lord Kinbote, and a mission to carry him to the center of the earth. The name is Nabokov's, from *Pale Fire*, and Cinefantastique called the creature a double tribute, to Nabokov and to Ray Harryhausen. There was no time or money for stop motion, so effects makeup supervisor Toby Lindala built a suit, and the footage was sped up and then slowed back down by computer to give it a jerky look. Morgan's own hope was that it "looked like stop-animation." The result is a practical effect processed to resemble an older and more obviously fake one, dropped into a scene lit and scored like any other monster reveal on the show. Morgan cited Nabokov's remark that "reality" should always have quotes around it as a direct influence, and the sped-up, slowed-down Kinbote footage is as close as a camera can come to the same mark.

## The body

The middle of the episode runs the show's own evidentiary procedures past the camera one piece at a time. Blaine, a UFO buff out looking for saucers, finds a dead alien in the woods, and Mulder, arriving at the body, lets out a high shriek that Meghan Deans at Reactor called glorious. When Detective Manners keeps his camcorder out of the autopsy, Blaine yells "Roswell!" twice, and again later when the men in black take his tape. Scully autopsies the corpse and finds an Air Force pilot in an alien costume. Her autopsy footage is then cut together and sold as a television special, *Dead Alien! Truth or Humbug?*, narrated by the Stupendous Yappi, the fake psychic from Morgan's *Clyde Bruckman's Final Repose*. Morgan was satirizing the alien autopsy tape that Fox, the show's own network, had put on the air, which he called a swindle. The joke has teeth because the series had spent three seasons treating an autopsy as the one scene in which Scully's method produced certainty. Here the method works, and within the hour the result is repackaged as the reverse of what it proved.

The men in black who come for Blaine's tape are played by Jesse Ventura and, more or less as himself, Alex Trebek. Morgan, talking to Kumail Nanjiani years later, said Trebek agreed on one condition, a bigger part, and had to be persuaded that the appearance worked because it was short. The scene is shot like every other government visitation in the series, two men in suits and a flat threat, and what breaks it is a face you know from a game show. A familiar face is the one cue the hour allows itself.

## Mulder in other people's stories

The structure is hardest on the leads. Mulder declines to be interviewed. He goes to see Chung to ask him not to write the book, and that is his last say in the matter. In Blaine's telling the agents are themselves the men in black, Mulder a "mandroid" and Scully a threat. In the version Chung got from the diner cook, which he reads back to Scully in her own interview, Mulder sits alone eating a whole sweet potato pie one slice at a time, asking UFO questions, and David Duchovny turns and flashes his badge straight into the lens. In Scully's version a pilot sat at that table and told Mulder everything. The show's hero becomes a figure in other people's testimony, and St. James suggested this may be the episode with the lowest opinion of Mulder of any in the series. Duchovny plays it with what Deans called extra pan in his deadpan.

Scully fares differently and, in the end, worse. She is the frame narrator, and the episode has her praise Chung's earlier novel, *The Caligarian Candidate*, as one of the greatest thrillers ever written, which Deans measured, on the Scully scale, as gushing. When the book is finished she and Mulder appear in it under altered names, and her closing assessment is that the case has more closure than some of their others. One of the two cues from this hour that La-La Land Records issued on its first X-Files set in 2011 is titled "Closure," and the music under the last montage is some of the gentlest Mark Snow wrote for the series. Asked by [Soundtrack.Net](https://www.eatthecorn.com/itw/1998/05/27/soundtrack-net/) in 1998 which episodes he had enjoyed scoring most, Snow would not pick one, but the first three that came to mind were this one, *Clyde Bruckman* and *Humbug*, all Darin Morgan's, before he added *Redux*.

## The montage

The ending gives every witness a last image. Roky has moved to El Cajon and preaches the inner earth and its lava men to the lost and desperate. Chrissy has decided her visitation was a message. Harold loves her and she does not love him back. Mulder lies alone in bed in his apartment watching Bigfoot footage that Morgan told Cinefantastique was phony. Chung types that evidence of extraterrestrial life remains as elusive as ever. Morgan was proud that the hour has a body count of two, with no death at an act break, and he did not want to end on a wacky note.

The sympathy and the rigor come from the same choice. By lighting every lie like the truth, Bowman and Morgan make it impossible to laugh at any one witness from a safe distance. Nobody in Klass County can prove what happened to them, the agents included, and the camera never takes a side. When the Television Academy listed the show's scariest episodes in October 2025, it picked two of Morgan's hours, *Humbug* and *Clyde Bruckman's Final Repose*, and passed over the one in which nothing at all can be verified.
