summary: Vancouver shot The X-Files dark because dark was cheap. Into that dark the show sent a handheld beam carried by a character, and for nine seasons the beam decided what the audience was allowed to know.
---
John S. Bartley lit the first three seasons of The X-Files to a stop of T2.8, interiors and night exteriors alike, and he did not pretend there was a theory behind it. "We didn't have any money, and that had a lot to do with the look," he told [the ASC](https://theasc.com/news/in-memoriam-john-s-bartley-asc-csc). He died in Los Angeles on August 17, 2025, at 78. Money explains the dark frame. It does not explain what the show put into the frame: a handheld xenon beam, carried by a character, pointed where the character chose to point it.

Almost everything else on the show lies. Witnesses contradict themselves, government men dissemble, the agents' own memories get rewritten. The beam cannot be made to lie. What it touches is real, and what it misses is unknown, so an hour of The X-Files plays as a first-person film that happens to be shot over the shoulder.

## Hard light, no fill

Bartley's method, as he described it to [Kodak's In Camera](https://www.eatthecorn.com/itw/1995/08/01/in-camera/) in 1995, was to underexpose and trust the stock. "With today's EXR films I've been five stops underexposed," he said. "I don't use much fill." Editors sometimes told him they could not see what was happening on their Avid monitors. Fox had asked for Super 35 so that the negative would be wide enough for high-definition syndication later, which is why the series could be reframed for widescreen two decades on. Mulder and Scully carried xenon flashlights, and in the darkest sets, the arctic submarine of *End Game* among them, those beams were the only source. Gaffers kicked the light back with Rosco pebble bounce so that it returned onto the actors' faces. A character holding a flashlight ends up lit by her own flashlight.

In conventional television the key light comes from somewhere the character cannot see: a lamp on a stand, a soft box above the lens. On The X-Files the key light is in the character's hand. Where the actor looks, the light goes, and where the light goes, the audience is permitted to see. Point of view and exposure become one decision.

> "I figured you out. You light the walls, but you don't light the actors."

A still photographer said that to Bartley on set, and Bartley told Kodak in 2011 that sometimes it was true. Walls, doorways and the far ends of corridors were lit; the agents found their light at the right moment or not at all. The show's silhouettes, a figure rimmed from behind in a doorway with nothing on the face, follow from that. A person is identified when a flashlight finds them, and until then they are a shape.

## Squeeze, Ice, Darkness Falls

In *Squeeze* and *Tooms* the threat lives in ventilation shafts and crawlspaces, and the camera does not go in after it. The flashlight does. We learn the geography of Eugene Tooms's world a cone at a time, and the horror is that the cone is small. *Ice*, the episode Bartley said "took the series to the next level," seals a research station into a box in which every character is a suspect, and the lighting stops distinguishing between the people the agents trust and the people they do not. In *Darkness Falls* the premise is the aesthetic made literal: the thing in the forest can only move in the dark, and the characters survive as long as a generator and a few bulbs hold out. For a year the show had treated light as the boundary of knowledge. Here it wrote an episode in which light is the boundary of life.

Mood on this show is the smoke and the backlight, the steam Bartley pumped into exteriors so that every source became a visible shaft. The flashlight does a different job. It marks the edge of what Mulder has established and what Scully has established, and when the two beams cross in a room their disagreement is staged as a composition: two cones of light sweeping the same space and settling on different objects.

Bartley left after season three. Joel Ransom, moved up from camera operator during the fourth season after Jon Joffin's short stay as cinematographer, carried the look through the end of the Vancouver years and earned an Emmy nomination for the black-and-white *Post-Modern Prometheus*. Bill Roe inherited it in Los Angeles. Directors came and went, and pacing, humor and the color palette all changed. The flashlight stayed, because a new cinematographer could no more drop it than drop Scully's skepticism. It belonged to the fiction.

## The monster in shadow

Bartley's own summary of the method was short: "We didn't reveal what's in the darkness." A monster movie that keeps its monster in shadow for the sake of it is cheating the audience. A monster movie in which the audience sees what the protagonist's flashlight sees, no more and no less, is playing fair. Every unseen thing is unseen for a reason that exists inside the story.

The prestige dramas that later borrowed the palette are dark because darkness reads as serious. The X-Files was dark because its characters were carrying their own light into places nobody else had been, and the camera admitted it could not see any further than they could.

## The awards

The approach was recognized at the time, which is rarer in television cinematography than it should be. Thomas Del Ruth's pilot brought him an ASC nomination in the movie-or-pilot category. Bartley was Emmy-nominated in 1995 for *One Breath* and won in 1996 for *Grotesque*, an episode that is almost entirely flashlights moving through an artist's studio full of gargoyles; the ASC nominated him three years running, 1995 through 1997, for *Duane Barry*, *731* and *Grotesque*. Ransom's Emmy nomination followed in 1998. Three cinematographers honored on one network series inside five years, before Roe added two ASC awards of his own in Los Angeles.

Chris Carter's brief, as the ASC recorded it, was that he liked it "dark, moody, mysterious and sometimes claustrophobic." Any competent crew could have delivered that. What the Vancouver unit delivered can be seen in the hour the Emmy went to: a studio full of clay gargoyles, two agents, two beams, and a face that stays unlit until a hand decides otherwise.
