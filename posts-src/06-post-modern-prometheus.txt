When *The Post-Modern Prometheus* aired on November 30, 1997, Chris Carter told The New York Times what he was after. The episode, which he wrote and directed, was shot in black and white as a tribute to the James Whale film "that starred Karloff," and he described his creature as an unlikely monster "who's got a fixation on Cher." Every account of the episode since has repeated the first half of that sentence and filed the hour under *Frankenstein* (1931).

Almost nothing in it behaves like the 1931 film. Whale's first *Frankenstein* is a grim, nearly humorless picture with a mute monster who ends it burning in a windmill. Carter's episode is a comedy with a monster who talks, a story told inside a frame, a scientist played for camp, a plea for companionship at its center and a finale that refuses the tragedy its plot has set up. Point for point, those are the marks of *Bride of Frankenstein* (1935), the sequel Whale made four years later. Karloff starred in both, so Carter's phrase covers either film. He may have been using the famous title as shorthand, or he may have had the sequel in his head all along. The episode reads much more easily with *Bride* beside it.

## The frame

*Bride* opens with its author. In a prologue set in 1818, Mary Shelley sits by a fire with Byron and her husband while a storm rages outside, and agrees to continue her story. Elsa Lanchester plays Shelley and, at the end of the film, the Bride, a doubling the American Society of Cinematographers' history of the film describes as Whale's way of showing that even sweet Mary harbored a monster within. The film announces itself as a story someone is telling.

Carter's episode opens the same way, translated into a 1990s idiom. The first image is a comic book, *The Great Mutato*, drawn by a teenager named Izzy, and the live action grows out of its panels. The final image returns to the comic. Everything between is framed as an authored object, and the episode never lets you forget it. The 1931 film has no such frame. It opens on a graveyard and means it.

The frame licenses the ending. Near the close, with the creature arrested and the plot heading toward a cell, Mulder protests that the story is ending wrong and demands to speak to the writer. The next scene is a Cher concert for the whole town, with the monster on stage and Mulder and Scully dancing. Critics have treated this as either a lark or a cheat. Within a story that announced itself as a story on page one, it is the author stepping in, as Whale's prologue promised an author could.

## The monster who speaks

In the 1931 film, Karloff's creature grunts. The ASC history records that Karloff thought it a mistake to let the monster speak in the sequel, because speech made him too sympathetic. Whale wanted the sympathy. He built the film's best sequence around a blind hermit who takes the creature in, feeds him and teaches him words, so that the monster can at last say what he wants, which is a friend.

The Great Mutato, played under heavy makeup by Chris Owens, is a hermit's pupil without the hermit. He has learned the world from Cher records and from *Mask* (1985), the film in which Cher plays the mother of a boy with a disfiguring condition. When he finally talks, in a long monologue to a crowd that came to kill him, he is articulate and gentle, and what he explains is that the man who raised him had been trying, by terrible means, to make him a mate. That is the plot of *Bride* reduced to a confession: a creature who wants not to be alone, and a maker who tries to manufacture the cure. Carter removes the Bride and substitutes the singer. Cher is the mate the monster cannot have, and the real Cher's absence, with an impersonator performing in the finale to Cher's own recordings, sharpens the joke. He gets the image of her, never the woman.

## The camp doctor

*Bride* is also where Whale's humor came off the leash. The ASC history has his taste for macabre comedy pushed to the limit in the sequel, with Ernest Thesiger's Dr. Pretorius, skeletal and arch, getting most of the film's best lines, many of them improvised on the set. Pretorius is a showman. He raises a beaker of gin "to a new world of gods and monsters" and enjoys himself.

John O'Hurley's Dr. Pollidori is Pretorius in a tweed jacket. He calls his own work visionary, concedes to Mulder that it could be done on human beings, and plays the exchange for vanity. Carter passed over Colin Clive's shaking, guilty Henry Frankenstein for the strut.

Pollidori is also a near spelling of John Polidori, the young physician who traveled with Byron to the Villa Diodati in 1816, sat in on the ghost-story contest that produced *Frankenstein*, and wrote *The Vampyre*, which the New Monthly Magazine printed in April 1819 as "A Tale by Lord Byron." Polidori is a figure from the summer *Bride* dramatizes in its prologue. He has no place in the 1931 film at all. Carter, or someone on his staff, was reading around the sequel.

## Joel Ransom's light

None of this makes the photography incidental. Joel Ransom was nominated for the cinematography Emmy in 1998, a nomination the ASC's own coverage of that year's awards ties to this hour, and the work deserves it because it refuses the easy version of monochrome. The temptation with black and white is to make everything a shadow. Ransom lights the small-town interiors brightly and flatly, like a 1950s sitcom, and saves the deep contrast for the barn, the woods and the mob. Whale and John Mescall did something similar in 1935, lighting the laboratory like an opera and the hermit's cottage like a nativity, and letting the two registers argue.

Black and white also gives the episode's talk-show material its bite. The townspeople want to be on Jerry Springer, who appears as himself, and their yearning plays as parody because the format they crave is so plainly a color medium. The title puts "post-modern" in front of Shelley's subtitle, and the joke is that the villagers with torches have been replaced by villagers with a story to sell.

## The 1998 ballot

The Television Academy records the show's 1998 Emmy entries by series, and the episode histories credit most of that year's to this hour: Carter's writing and directing nominations, Ransom's photography, Mark Snow's score, the makeup team, and the art direction award the show won. The Directors Guild nominated Carter for the episode by name. It was recognized as a piece of filmmaking, and the habit of filing it under "homage to *Frankenstein*" has let it be admired without being read.

The finale, which sends the monster to a concert instead of a windmill, is the ending *Bride* could not give its creature. Whale's monster pulls a lever and brings the tower down on himself and the woman who screamed at him. Carter's gets Cher. It is sentimental, and it is also an argument, made in black and white, that the 1935 picture wanted a happy ending and that television, sixty-two years later, could afford one. The one award the hour took home went to Graeme Murray, Greg Loewen and Shirley Inget, for the sets.
