summary: Chris Carter built Triangle on Hitchcock's Rope, and the comparison has stuck. The episode's best act is a ten-minute chase through FBI headquarters that plays as farce, timed by a Steadicam instead of an editor.
---
*Triangle* arrived on November 22, 1998 wrapped in a famous name. Chris Carter, who wrote and directed it, built the episode on Alfred Hitchcock's *Rope*: four acts, each designed to play as one unbroken take, a ghost liner adrift in the Bermuda Triangle, Mulder trapped in 1939 among Nazis. The Directors Guild nominated Carter for it, his third nomination from the show after *The List* and *The Post-Modern Prometheus*, and the Hitchcock comparison has stuck to the hour ever since. Hitchcock means suspense.

The long take in *Triangle* does almost nothing for dread. It does comic timing, superbly. The episode's best act is a chase through an office building that plays like a Blake Edwards set piece, and its emotional payoff is a punch in the mouth followed by a joke. Carter borrowed Hitchcock's method and found, probably without meaning to, that on television the unbroken shot belongs to farce.

## Rope, 1948

*Rope* was shot in takes limited by how much film a camera could hold, a little over ten minutes. The film has eleven shots and ten cuts, and Chris Wisniewski, writing in Reverse Shot, lays out the pattern: every other cut is an ordinary edit, placed where projectionists would have to change reels anyway, and the ones in between are hidden by pushing the lens into a man's back until the frame goes dark. Hitchcock wanted you to feel the clock, and the visible cuts helped. So did the painted skyline behind the actors, which goes from afternoon to night across the film's eighty minutes and makes the evening feel longer than it is. *Rope* uses continuous time to tighten a screw.

*Triangle* has the same constraint in a different form. Bill Roe, the show's new director of photography that season, told Kodak's Bob Fisher that every shot in the episode was on a Steadicam, because everything was supposed to be happening in real time, and that "it seems like every shot was four minutes long." Dave Luckenbach operated the rig for ten straight days. The fan archive EatTheCorn, in a [2025 post summarizing an X-Files Diaries podcast interview](https://www.eatthecorn.com/tag/triangle/), has Carter saying the footage added up to the forty-five minutes the hour needed with nothing to spare, and that setting the story at night helped hide the joins. The question is what the continuous camera is doing while it runs.

## The Queen Anne

Act one puts Mulder aboard the *Queen Anne*, a liner that vanished in 1939 and has surfaced in 1998 with its original passengers still aboard. The production shot on the real Queen Mary in Long Beach, dressed for the period, and the ship gives the Steadicam long corridors and real stairwells to work with. It should be ideal.

It isn't. Suspense in a long take comes from a space the viewer has learned. In *Rope*, one apartment, one evening, one trunk. On the ship, Mulder is running through rooms he has never seen toward a goal he is improvising, pursued by a Nazi crew led by the Cigarette Smoking Man, with Spender as his lieutenant and Skinner as a sympathetic officer. The camera follows at a jog, and because it never stops to establish geography, the geography never accumulates. We don't know how far the engine room is from the ballroom, so there is no dread in the distance. Carter's doubled casting, which gives the regulars 1939 counterparts, is a pleasure, but it is a dream's pleasure. Meghan Deans, reviewing the episode for Reactor, reads the 1939 story as something Mulder made up while passed out in the ocean, with the *Wizard of Oz* references to prove it. Dreams can be funny. They are rarely suspenseful, because nothing in them is at stake in a way the body believes.

## The Hoover Building

Then act two cuts to Washington, and the episode becomes a different thing. Scully needs one piece of information, the location of a ship, and she has to extract it from a building full of people who will not help her. The camera attaches itself to her back and does not let go as she goes from Kersh's office to Spender to the Lone Gunmen to Skinner, up and down floors, in and out of elevators, through a maze of standing sets the Los Angeles crew had only just built.

The continuity here compresses time, the way a farce does. Scully's lines are quick, her turns are sharp, and every obstacle arrives on a beat. The elevator becomes a running gag: Carter, in the same interview, describes the set being redressed while the doors were closed so that the same box could open onto a different scene, an old stage trick dressed as cinema. When Scully gets what she needs she grabs Skinner and kisses him, an act so out of character that it only works because the camera has been with her, breathless, for ten unbroken minutes and has earned the release. Emily St. James, in The A.V. Club, called this her favorite act, and what she singled out was Gillian Anderson, "so delightful, racing all over the building."

Hitchcock's long take asks you to wait; Carter's asks you to keep up, which has been the business of physical comedy since Keaton: a body moving through space in real time, with no cut to save it from its own momentum.

## The split screen

The last two acts divide the frame, 1939 on one side and 1998 on the other, and the device's celebrated moment is the one where the two Scullys pass each other at the center line, pause, and frown. People describe this as uncanny, and it is, slightly. It is also a double take. The timing is a comedian's: the pass, the half-beat, the shared look of puzzlement, the resumption. Two moments in time pressed against each other, the split screen's grand subject, arrives as a joke about mistaken identity, the oldest joke farce has.

The ending keeps the register. Mulder, convinced he is about to die in 1939, kisses the OSS agent who looks like his partner. She punches him. He wakes in a hospital bed, tells the real Scully he loves her, and she answers "Oh, brother." The episode has been working toward a declaration for forty minutes, and it lands as a punchline with the long-take machinery as the setup. Deans sees the romance reduced to a running joke by that line. I would say Carter knew what kind of hour he had made and ended it in kind.

## Comedy on a schedule

Calling *Triangle* a comedy explains why it is remembered with affection rather than awe, and why the one Emmy nomination credited to it was for sound editing, while the directing nomination came from the guild. It also says something about the long take as a television tool. Suspense in a long take depends on a learned space and a slow clock, which a weekly schedule rarely allows. Comedy in a long take depends on choreography and actors who can hit marks for minutes at a stretch, which a trained series cast can deliver. Carter had Anderson, a Steadicam operator with ten days of stamina, and a set of corridors, and he used them for what they were good at.

Hitchcock's camera watches a crime cool in a trunk. Carter's runs after a woman who refuses to stop. The Directors Guild put him on its ballot for 1998 all the same, next to Steve Buscemi for an hour of *Homicide* and Paris Barclay for *NYPD Blue*, in the category it calls Dramatic Series Night.
