summary: Handed the director's chair, David Duchovny and Gillian Anderson each made the same decision. They removed the other one. What the four episodes say about a partnership seen from inside.
---
The usual reading of the episodes David Duchovny and Gillian Anderson directed is that his are playful and fond of genre, hers is inward and slow, and together they show two temperaments. That is true and it is not very interesting. The more useful observation is what the four hours have in common. Every one of them is built by removing the other lead.

Duchovny's *The Unnatural* confines Scully to two scenes and spends its middle in 1947, where neither agent exists. His *Hollywood A.D.* replaces the partnership with a parody of it, played by other actors. His *William*, which he directed in the ninth season, has Mulder in it only briefly. Anderson's *all things* puts Mulder on a plane to England in the first act and leaves Scully alone in Washington. The actors who had spent six or seven years in a two-shot each used the chair to find out what the show was when the two-shot was gone. The answers were different, and that difference is the real subject.

## Duchovny: the self in other bodies

Duchovny told Cinefantastique that he directed *The Unnatural*, which aired April 25, 1999, partly to protect his script, admitting he had sometimes been disappointed in the show's execution of material he had a hand in. He had shared story credit with Carter on *Colony*, *Anasazi* and *Talitha Cumi*, mythology episodes that belong to the house style. This time he wanted his own vision carried through, and he described directing as "a part of the writing process."

The vision is a period fable. Arthur Dales tells Mulder about Josh Exley, a Black ballplayer for the Roswell Grays in 1947 who is secretly an alien, and the hour lives in the flashbacks. Darren McGavin, who had played Dales, fell ill days into the shoot, so M. Emmet Walsh stepped in as the character's brother and Fredric Lehne played the younger Dales. Jesse L. Martin carries the episode; Duchovny discovered during production that Martin had a beautiful singing voice and rewrote to put him in a scene singing. Bill Roe's warm photography, a deliberate inversion of the show's palette, earned the series one of its 1999 Emmy nominations, for cinematography.

Scully is present for an opening argument over a fat-free ice cream cone and a closing batting lesson at night. Duchovny gave her the last line, "Shut up, I'm playing baseball," and said the lesson Mulder was supposed to have learned was simply the value of loving the game. The partner is reduced to a frame, and the emotional center is handed to a surrogate, a man who hides what he is in order to be allowed to play. Exley is Mulder with the longing made literal, and the Klan riders who open the episode are the men in suits made visible.

*Hollywood A.D.*, which aired a year later on April 30, 2000, takes the next step and removes the partnership by duplicating it. A producer, played by comic Wayne Federman, shadows the agents on a church-bombing case and turns it into a film in which Garry Shandling plays Mulder and Téa Leoni, Duchovny's wife at the time, plays Scully. Carter and Minnie Driver sit in the audience at the premiere. The structural centerpiece is a three-way split screen of Mulder, Scully and Skinner each in a hotel bubble bath, a phone call staged, as Cinefantastique noted, in the manner of *Pillow Talk*. The episode's zombies come to life on the soundstage and tango after the real agents have left; Duchovny said the story "happens behind their backs."

He also told the Seattle Times the episode was "probably darker than just about any show we've ever done," and that what he was after was the play of realness and fakeness across several levels at once. That is a strange claim about a comedy with dancing corpses, until you see what he gave Scully: a resurrection story, religious fanatics, and a test of belief she has to pass alone. Even here, the partner is interesting to him as a problem of faith, not as a presence in the room. The real Mulder and Scully spend the climax hiding on a standing set while the plot resolves without them.

*William*, which aired April 28, 2002, completes the pattern. Duchovny directed it from a story credited to him, Carter and Frank Spotnitz, with Carter writing the teleplay. A disfigured man appears in the X-Files office, Doggett tests his DNA, and the results point toward Mulder, who is otherwise absent. The episode is about a Mulder who is nowhere, investigated by people who never knew him, and it ends with Scully giving up their son. The actor spent his last directing job on the show staging his own disappearance.

## Anderson: the self alone

Anderson's single episode, *all things*, aired April 9, 2000, three weeks before *Hollywood A.D.*. It was the first hour of the series directed by a woman, and the only one a woman both wrote and directed. It behaves like the work of someone who has waited a long time to be allowed to make a decision.

The first decision is the opening. Scully dresses in a dim bedroom while Mulder sleeps a few feet away, an image the show had spent seven years not giving its audience, delivered as a flash-forward before a word of plot. The second is to send Mulder to England to chase crop circles, which the hour treats as a joke, and to let Scully carry the rest. A chain of coincidences leads her to Daniel Waterston, played by Nicolas Surovy, a married doctor she had an affair with in medical school. He collapses; his daughter still hates her; a woman Scully follows down the street leads her into a Buddhist temple, where she has a vision of the life she did not choose.

The technique is unlike anything else in the series. Anderson shoots Scully walking to a Moby track. She drops a stack of papers in slow motion so that a page about the heart chakra can land face up. The camera stays on a curtain cord knocking against a wall. The A.V. Club, which gave the hour a C, said it "wants to be a poem" and keeps getting dragged back into the formula it is trying to escape, while allowing that the nerve behind it was real even where the dialogue was not. It ends with Scully telling Mulder the whole story and falling asleep while he is still talking about fate.

Where Duchovny kept finding Mulder in other bodies, Anderson refused to find Scully anywhere but Scully. No surrogates, no parody, no period costume. The partner is removed to make room for silence. It is the only episode of the original run in which Scully's attention, rather than a case, is the organizing principle, and the formal choices, the slow motion, the lingering, the pop song, are all ways of showing attention itself.

## What they knew

Kim Manners, who directed more of the series than anyone, told Fangoria in 2000 that the two stars had given so much of their personal lives to the show that they had earned the right to direct. The investment shows in what they chose to test. Duchovny tested whether the show's ideas could survive being handed to other people: a ballplayer, a comedian, a stranger in the basement office. Anderson tested whether one of its two people could hold the screen without the argument that had defined her.

Neither test was flattering to the format. The series had sold itself for years on the two-shot, on the belief that the show was the conversation between these two people. Its own leads, given the camera, each quietly suggested otherwise: that Mulder was a set of ideas that could be reassigned, and that Scully was a consciousness that did not need him in the room. The partnership survived, and the show went on for two more seasons. But the four hours remain the clearest record of how the people inside the two-shot understood it, and the answer was: separately.
