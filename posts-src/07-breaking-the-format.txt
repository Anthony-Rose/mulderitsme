title: Who Holds the Camera: The X-Files' Real Format Experiments
date: 2026-10-16
tag: Form
hue: 275
summary: The show's famous rule-breaking episodes get lumped together as stunts, but they are two different projects. One asserts the director's control of the image. The other, the more radical one, hands the camera to someone who can't be trusted.
---
Ask an X-Files viewer to name the episodes that broke the format and the same titles come back: *Jose Chung's From Outer Space*, *Bad Blood*, *Triangle*, *The Post-Modern Prometheus*, *Monday*, *X-Cops*, and from the revival, *Rm9sbG93ZXJz*. They are discussed as one tradition, the show letting its hair down. The grouping hides a split that matters more than anything they share.

Two of those hours, *Triangle* and *The Post-Modern Prometheus*, were written and directed by Chris Carter, and they are director's episodes in the fullest sense: a Steadicam held for minutes at a stretch, a palette drained to black and white. They assert control. The camera knows everything and shows you exactly what the author decides. The other five do the opposite. Each hands the camera, or the account the camera is illustrating, to a narrator who is partial, confused, repeating herself, following a format she did not choose, or not human. The X-Files was a show about the unreliability of testimony. Its deepest formal experiments were the ones that made the episode itself a piece of testimony, and this family, not Carter's, is the one that taught television comedy a grammar it still uses.

## The witness who wasn't there

Darin Morgan's *Jose Chung's From Outer Space*, directed by Rob Bowman and first aired on April 12, 1996, is the founding document. A novelist, played by Charles Nelson Reilly, is researching a book about an abduction, and the episode is built from the interviews he conducts. A teenager's account under hypnosis describes aliens; a second session replaces them with Air Force men who erase her memory. A man named Roky insists he saw a creature from the earth's core he calls Lord Kinbote. A UFO obsessive describes Mulder and Scully as men in black. Scully gives her own version to Chung, and even she cannot account for what Mulder did at a diner.

What makes this a format experiment rather than a clever script is where the camera sits. Every scene we see is somebody's claim. The show's standard episode had a single trustworthy eye; the camera showed a monster, and the agents' job was to catch up. Morgan removed the trustworthy eye. He kept the show's furniture, the hypnotist, the eyewitness, the government men, and made all of it equally dubious, so that the hour ends with Chung at his typewriter observing that on this planet "we are all alone." The A.V. Club's Emily St. James called it one of the finest episodes of television ever made while doubting it was a great episode of The X-Files. That doubt is the point. It is an episode about what the show's camera had been pretending to know.

## Two partners, one night

Vince Gilligan's *Bad Blood*, directed by Cliff Bole and aired February 22, 1998, takes Morgan's move and makes it intimate. Mulder has staked a Texas teenager he believed was a vampire. On the way up to Skinner's office the partners rehearse their stories, and we see the case twice, first as Scully remembers it and then as Mulder does. The events barely differ. Scully's version has a fast-talking, insufferable Mulder and a handsome sheriff, played by Luke Wilson, who takes an interest in her science. Mulder's version gives the same sheriff an overbite and has Mulder patiently explaining vampire lore to a partner who will not listen. Whether Scully's shots hit the pizza boy changes depending on who is telling it.

The craft here is in the control of mismatch. Each retelling has to be the same scene, same set, same blocking, with the acting recalibrated. Gillian Anderson plays Scully as the put-upon adult in her own account and as a sulking skeptic in Mulder's, and the comedy lives in the gap. Zack Handlen, reviewing it for The A.V. Club, observed that, unlike *Rashomon*, the two accounts barely differ on events; the differences are in the details. The camera has stopped recording the world and started recording self-regard. That is a more unsettling idea than any monster, and the show delivered it as farce.

## The one who remembers

*Monday*, written by Gilligan and John Shiban and directed by Kim Manners, aired February 28, 1999. Mulder's waterbed leaks, he needs to deposit a paycheck, and he walks into a bank where a robber named Bernard is wearing a bomb. The day repeats. Mulder does not know it. The only person who does is Bernard's girlfriend, Pam, who has lived this Monday more times than she can count and has stopped believing it can change.

Here the unreliable eye becomes an exhausted one. The camera is loyal to the agents' experience, which is why each loop looks like a first time, but the episode's truth belongs to Pam, who sits outside the bank watching variations the agents cannot perceive. Handlen contrasted her with Bill Murray in *Groundhog Day*, who passes through disbelief and indulgence toward growth; Pam is stuck in despair, with six hours at most per loop and no leverage over the outcome. The loop ends only when she steps in front of a bullet meant for Mulder. Structurally, the episode is *Bad Blood* with the second narrator removed: we are shown one version repeatedly and told, by the one person who knows, that the version is a lie.

## The format that wasn't ours

Gilligan had reportedly been pitching *X-Cops* since the fourth season, and when it aired on February 20, 2000, directed by Michael Watkins, the show handed its camera to another show entirely. The agents are followed through a night in Los Angeles by a documentary crew, in the house style of *COPS*. Gilligan told [the Television Academy](https://www.televisionacademy.com/features/online-originals/x-files-vince-gilligan-x-cops-episode) that he wanted it to look as much like *COPS* as possible, so it was shot on video rather than 35mm, and that Bertram van Munster, a *COPS* producer and lead camera operator, shot a fair amount of it himself. Watkins told Entertainment Weekly before it aired that the commitment was "to be Cops no matter what."

The reality format has rules the show had never obeyed: no music, no cutaways, available light, the camera as a participant the subjects can see. Scully spends the hour visibly resenting the crew. The creature, which seems to take the shape of each victim's fear, is barely glimpsed, because the format cannot show what the operator did not catch, and a sketch artist's rendering of it looks like Freddy Krueger. The joke doubles as the method. The X-Files had always withheld its monsters by choice. *X-Cops* withholds them by rule, and the rule belongs to someone else. Handlen argued that the flat, shaky footage gets past the viewer's defenses precisely because it does not look like the show.

## The eye that isn't a person

The revival's experiment came from the same lineage, though not from Gilligan. *Rm9sbG93ZXJz*, written by Kristen Cloke and Shannon Hamblin and directed by Glen Morgan, aired at the end of February 2018. Cloke has said in [podcast interviews](https://www.eatthecorn.com/tag/rm9-followers/) that Morgan gave the writers four required elements: drones, artificial intelligence, Microsoft's Tay chatbot, and no dialogue. The result opens with a computer voice narrating the fate of a chatbot that learned too much from its followers, then puts Mulder and Scully in an automated sushi bar, empty except for them, where Mulder refuses to tip and the machines decline to accept the decision. The title is base64 for "Followers."

Morgan shoots much of it from the machines' point of view, from security cameras and drones, in clean, polished spaces. The camera is finally not a witness at all. It is the antagonist, and it is watching the agents the way the show once watched its monsters. Handlen described the episode as playing like a silent-film comedy, and the comparison is exact: without speech, the hour depends on two actors who have twenty-five years of physical shorthand, and on an audience trained to read their faces in the dark.

## The two traditions

Put the five together and the pattern is plain. A novelist's informants, two rival partners, a woman trapped in a day, a reality crew, a network of appliances: each episode names the eye through which we are seeing and makes that eye the problem. Carter's experiments, for all their skill, never do this. The long take and the black and white are an author showing you his hand. The writers' episodes hide the hand and ask whose it is.

That second project is the one with descendants. The comic procedural of the last two decades, with its mockumentary crews, its contradictory flashbacks and its episodes narrated by the least reliable person in the room, runs on a grammar The X-Files worked out in these hours by turning its subject on itself. A show about witnesses who could not be trusted eventually asked whether its own camera could be, and the honest answer, delivered as comedy, was no.
