title: Fight the Future Is Best When It Forgets It Is a Movie
date: 2026-10-16
tag: The Films
hue: 12
summary: The 1998 film spent its money on a spaceship under the ice and a helicopter over a cornfield. Its best scenes cost almost nothing, and they are the ones that still look like The X-Files.
---
*The X-Files* opened on June 19, 1998, at number one, with $30.1 million on 2,629 screens, and it was engineered to leave its story where it found it. The film sits between the fifth and sixth seasons. Mulder and Scully end it reassigned to the X-Files, the conspiracy intact, the colonization date unmoved. When the DVD arrived in 2001, Salon's Jeff Stark wrote that the picture "comes off like an episode of the television show with a really big budget," which, he allowed, was not a terrible thing. He meant it as a limitation. I think it names what is good about the film, and that the film is weakest at the moments it tries hardest to disprove the charge.

Later known by the subtitle *Fight the Future*, the picture is a good two-hour episode with a blockbuster grafted onto it. The sequences that enlarge the series, the Antarctic ice station, the craft beneath it, the helicopter chase through the corn, are where it goes slack and generic. The sequences that behave like television, a hallway, a bar, an alley, a federal building with a bomb in a vending machine, are where it is alive. The film was conceived to prove the show could scale, and its best scenes are the ones that refuse to.

## Built to change nothing

Chris Carter wrote the screenplay from a story he developed with Frank Spotnitz, and the production ran under the code name Blackwood with a crew largely drawn from the series. According to [American Cinematographer](https://theasc.com/magazine/jul98/elusive/pg1.htm)'s July 1998 production story, many top Hollywood directors expressed interest, and Carter wanted Rob Bowman anyway: a producer-director who had been with the show since its first season, who had 23 episodes behind him, more than anyone else, and in whom Carter had planted the idea of a film back in season three.

The continuity was a commercial bet, and it paid: $83.9 million domestic, $189.2 million worldwide, on a budget reported at [$60 million](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/x-files-tops-weekend-box-office/) by the wire services on opening weekend and at $66 million by The Numbers today. It also meant the film could not do what features do that television cannot, which is end. A movie that must hand its characters back unchanged can only enlarge the middle. So the money went to the middle.

## Motivated light, moving camera

Bowman and his cinematographer, Ward Russell, agreed on "motivated lighting effects, an active camera, and restrained use of color," as Russell put it to Live Design. The first and third are the series' own rules. Russell was a late hire; prep started so late, according to American Cinematographer, that a lot of top feature cinematographers had already signed on to their summer projects. Russell had worked as a gaffer under Tony Scott's cameraman Jeffrey Kimball before photographing *Days of Thunder* and *The Last Boy Scout* for Scott, and he kept the show's darkness, lighting even bright scenes by surrounding the foreground with black. He recommended anamorphic; the production chose Super 35 for budget reasons and shot almost everything on Kodak's 500-speed 5279 for a schedule heavy with night work. The widescreen frame came from cropping a spherical image, so the result is a 2.35:1 picture that still composes like a television one.

The Dallas sequence is the demonstration. Mulder and Scully are pulled off a bomb threat and sent to search the wrong building. The scene is a corridor, a flashlight, a vending machine, and a man who decides to stay with the bomb. It is composed the way the series composed its best hours: a hard single source, black at the edges, two agents arguing about what they are looking at. It is the most frightening passage in the film, and the cheapest.

The hallway outside Mulder's apartment works the same way. Scully announces she is quitting. Mulder tells her she saved him, that he does not want to do this without her. The camera barely moves. The two actors stand a foot apart in a frame that could have been shot on any Tuesday in Vancouver. The scene ends with a bee sting instead of a kiss, the show's oldest joke: the thing you want is interrupted by the thing the plot needs. Nothing in the film's last forty minutes lands as hard as that interruption.

## The cornfield and the ice

Then the film leaves the building. The Texas cornfields under their white domes are a genuine image, but the helicopter chase that follows is a sequence any action unit could have shot, and it looks like it. Storyboard artist Gabriel Hardman, interviewed on the *Hey Danny It's Mulder* podcast, has described the planning: the hive interiors needed wide lenses to show scale, and the bees ended up computer-generated. The picture was overfull of the material it was least suited to.

Antarctica is the proof. The craft under the ice is a large, well-built set, and the sequence is competently staged, but the series' first principle, that you never quite see the thing, is abandoned. We see the thing. We see the ship rise through the ice in a wide shot held for seconds. The moment that was supposed to pay off five seasons of restraint is the moment the film becomes indistinguishable from every other effects picture of 1998. The frame that had been composed around absence is suddenly full, and the series had never once made fullness frightening.

Russell said that very seldom was anything static; even on a dolly the camera was usually moving. That is a Tony Scott habit, and it serves the Dallas search and the alley scenes with Martin Landau's jittery informant. In the third act the camera keeps moving because nothing in the composition is being held back.

## Landau, Danner, Mueller-Stahl

The casting shows the same split. Landau's Kurtzweil belongs to the show's tradition of nervous informants, and his scenes with Duchovny, in a bar and a dark alley, are television scenes: two men and a secret, with the street behind them out of focus. Blythe Danner's Jana Cassidy, running the hearings that bracket the film, is a bureaucrat shot the way the series shot Skinner's superiors, with institutional light and no warmth. Armin Mueller-Stahl's Strughold is a movie villain, introduced at the Syndicate's London meeting and dispatched to a Tunisian cornfield for the coda. His is the casting that feels imported from another picture, and his scenes could be cut without loss.

## The sound off

The second film, a decade later, went small. The lesson was available in 1998 to anyone who watched *Fight the Future* with the sound off. The sequences that read as X-Files are dark, close and nearly motionless. The ones that read as summer product are bright and wide and never stop moving. For two hours the show's way of seeing and the blockbuster's run side by side, and the show wins every round it is allowed to fight.

Hardman's storyboards also held a car chase modeled on *The French Connection*, Mulder pursuing the ambulance carrying Scully away, which the script called "the car chase of Rob Bowman's dreams." It never made it to the screen. It was cut, Hardman said, because "there was too much in the movie already."
