title: Los Angeles Made the Look a Choice

summary: In the summer of 1998 The X-Files left the rain for hard sun. The four Los Angeles seasons went on to collect two ASC awards and two Emmy nominations, and a photography that had arrived with the weather became something the crew had to build shot by shot.
---
*The Beginning*, the sixth-season premiere, opens on a desert under a white sky, and nothing in the first five years of The X-Files had looked like it. Production had left Vancouver that summer for Los Angeles. The rain went, the fog went, the forests went, and in their place came hard sun, desert highways and the beige of Southern California tract housing. Longtime viewers have dated the end of the show's atmosphere to that move ever since.

For five seasons in British Columbia the look was largely a gift of climate. It was a magnificent gift and the crew that received it was superb, but overcast skies, wet asphalt and real mist turn up whether or not anyone asks for them. In Los Angeles the photography had to be chosen, shot by shot, against an environment that offered nothing for free. Those four seasons produced the most consistently honored cinematography of the show's run, and a visual identity that belonged to the production and no longer to the Pacific Northwest.

## Vancouver, March 1998

Chris Carter never pretended otherwise. Announcing the move to the [Vancouver Sun](https://mjq.net/xfiles/cc-farewell.htm) on March 30, 1998, he said, "I've always felt that Vancouver was one of the stars of the show," and that leaving its crew was the hardest part of the decision. Later that year he put the practical side to the [Los Angeles Times](https://www.eatthecorn.com/?p=1941) with unusual candor. "Before, we had rain and misty conditions," he said. "Now we'll have to make them, without it looking forced."

Flat, soft daylight with no hard shadows is forgiving to shoot in and matches from setup to setup across an afternoon. Rain turns a night street into a field of reflections, so that a single practical lamp lights a whole block. Mist turns every source into a visible shaft. Vancouver handed those effects to the show on most shooting days, and John S. Bartley, the first three seasons' cinematographer, built a dark, low-fill style on top of them that won him an Emmy in 1996. The achievement was real. Its raw material arrived with the weather.

The move was driven by David Duchovny, who had told talk-show hosts the previous year that he would sooner quit the series than spend another year away from his wife, the actress Téa Leoni, and that keeping him meant moving to Los Angeles. Carter denied he had been pressured, though he acknowledged promising both stars that the show would not stay in Vancouver indefinitely.

## Hard sun

Rob Bowman, who had directed more than two dozen episodes and had just finished the feature film, saw the problem before the crew arrived. "You're not going to get the diffuse light every day," he told Sci-Fi Age in its November 1998 issue; what they would get instead was "hard sun" and "LA sun." His answer was to find "a new look that takes the old one and goes a little further with it." Post-production supervisor Paul Rabwin told the Times that "the colors here have a whole different hue," and that the camera department had switched film stocks and cameras to cope.

The person who had to deliver was Bill Roe, a camera veteran who had shot one season of *Brooklyn South* as director of photography. According to a Kodak profile of Roe, his mentor Michael Watkins, for whom Roe had operated camera, joined the show as a producer in Los Angeles and introduced him to Carter. Roe stayed through the end of the ninth season. His first episode with Bowman, *Drive*, shows what the Los Angeles show could do that the Vancouver show could not. Most of it takes place in daylight inside a moving car on an open highway, with a hostage, a dying man and no cover. There is nowhere to hide a monster and no darkness to keep it in. The suspense is built from the geometry of the road, the dust, the heat and the color of the light as the day runs out, and the ASC gave Roe its award for it in 1999. He won again the following year for *Agua Mala*, a hurricane episode drenched in moving water. In Vancouver that would have been another rainy night. In Los Angeles it had to be manufactured from nothing.

> "Now we'll have to make them, without it looking forced."

Then came the episodes that could only exist in the new light. *The Unnatural*, Duchovny's writing and directing debut, is a 1940s baseball story shot in warm, late-afternoon tones that look like no other hour of the series. Roe told American Cinematographer that he hung lamps from cranes and swung them so the sunlight would seem to shift as a bus traveled, and that the ball field built in Simi Valley was lit by period light standards mounted on the backs of five pickup trucks. "In those days, the generators wouldn't sync, so the lights would pulse a little bit," he said, and the crew put the flicker back in on purpose. *Triangle*, which Carter shot in long continuous takes aboard the Queen Mary in Long Beach, needed a real ocean liner within driving distance of the studio. The Television Academy nominated Roe in 1999, for *The Unnatural*, and again in 2001. In four seasons the Los Angeles unit collected two ASC awards and two Emmy nominations. The Vancouver unit, in five, collected one Emmy win and a handful of nominations. Both records are excellent, and the second was earned in hard sun.

## Arcadia and the surfaces

Season six is often said to have gone soft, the comedies and the romantic undercurrent arriving with the sunshine. The light may have led and the tone followed. Carter told the Times that the city would force the show to "tell stories using the landscape that is presented to us now," and the landscape of Los Angeles is one of surfaces: tract housing, strip malls, planned communities. *Arcadia*, set in a gated development with a homeowners' association that kills, is a Los Angeles story in a way no Vancouver episode could have been. If the show stopped being about what hides in the dark, cheering up had little to do with it. Plain sight was what the camera had now, so plain sight was where things hid.

## The 2015 homecoming

When Fox announced a six-episode revival on March 24, 2015, production returned to Vancouver, shooting there from June 8 to September 3. A ten-episode eleventh season was ordered in April 2017 and shot there too. Fox's Dana Walden told critics in January 2016 that the revival had been short because of the stars' schedules: "Gillian lives in the U.K. David lives in New York and California and we do the show in Vancouver." The homecoming was the right sentimental choice, and the revival episodes look handsome. They also look, at first glance, like any well-shot drama filmed in the city today. The Vancouver atmosphere was available to anyone with a permit. The Los Angeles look had to be built, and it stayed with the show that built it.

One caution for anyone testing this argument on a screen. The series was shot in Super 35 and broadcast in 4:3, and the HD remaster, which reached Netflix in 2015 and Blu-ray that December, was scanned from the original negatives and reframed to 16:9, adding image at the sides and losing some at the top and bottom. Bartley told Kodak in 2011 that "we protected for 16:9, because we knew it was coming," and the remaster bears him out. The compositions on a modern screen are close to what the cinematographers shot, and not always the ones the directors chose. The one episode whose 4:3 image had to be cropped to fit the wider window is the pilot.
