summary: Glen Morgan and James Wong named a sheriff after Andy Griffith's and then held him to Mayberry habits: no gun, no locks, nothing changes. Home kills him by those rules and gives the Peacocks his nostalgia, down to the song on the car radio.
---
Mulder is tossing a baseball at the edge of a sandlot, telling Scully that if he ever settled down it would be in a town like this. "Be like living in Mayberry," she says. A pickup pulls in, a man walks under the crime-scene tape and puts out his hand: "Hi, I'm Sheriff Andy Taylor." Mulder asks, "For real?" A scene later the sheriff introduces his deputy, Barney. "Fife?" Mulder tries, and the deputy barks his own surname and stalks off. *Home* aired on Fox on October 11, 1996, the second episode of the fourth season, written by Glen Morgan and James Wong and directed by Kim Manners. Morgan laughed about the names to Paula Vitaris of [Cinefantastique](https://www.eatthecorn.com/itw/1997/10/01/cinefantastique-4/) the following year. "We had to do that!"

The names play as a gag, and then the script holds the sheriff to them. [PolitiFact](https://politifact.com/factchecks/2013/apr/08/b-heath-mitchell/it-takes-lot-live-mayberry/), checking a Mayberry comparison in 2013, put the premise of the 1960s sitcom in two clauses: Griffith's sheriff "didn't carry a gun," and his deputy "was allowed just one bullet." Tucker Smallwood's Taylor tells the agents in his first scene, "I don't even wear a gun." With a dead infant in the station refrigerator he still will not lock his office, because folks know he never does and would start rumors.

Emily St. James, in the *Home* chapter of *Monsters of the Week*, [excerpted at Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/tv/the-x-files-darkest-episode-revisited/), reads the hour as a creepy-house horror film and an elegy for a weird, local America that the agents' modern world is flattening. The elegy is there, but the script is harder on it than that. Morgan and Wong build the central killing out of the sheriff's two habits, and they hand the killers his own wish that nothing in Home ever change. By the last act the family across the field looks like the most faithful Mayberry household in the county.

## Brother's Keeper, and a note from Standards

The Peacocks came from two places. Kristen Cloke, the female lead of Morgan and Wong's *Space: Above and Beyond*, pointed the writers to *Brother's Keeper*, the 1992 documentary about brothers on a farm in upstate New York, in what Vitaris says was the county where Morgan's family lived when he was a boy. The mother came out of Charlie Chaplin's autobiography, from his memory of a landlord's legless son, Gilbert, who crawled out from under a dresser. Morgan first pictured three of his *Space* actors as the brothers. Fox's Standards department balked at the story and then approved it on a condition: the brothers could not resemble anyone a viewer might meet. They had to look like monsters, and by Morgan's telling the network "didn't want the Peacocks to talk." Under prosthetics and without lines, the parts no longer suited his actors. Manners and Rob Bowman pushed the other way. "The guys should talk," Morgan recalled them saying, and he refused: "They just come out with a club."

That ruling divides the hour by sound. The town talks constantly. Mulder and Scully banter on a bench about genetics, in a motel about the Knicks, in a pigpen about *Babe*, and the Taylors murmur on a porch. From the brothers there are grunts. A family with almost no dialogue needs something to speak for it, and Morgan had already picked the voice.

## The Cadillac's radio

Morgan wanted "Wonderful! Wonderful!" from the beginning, went through about fifty other songs, and came back to it. "It's too wonderful," he said. It was Johnny Mathis's first Columbia single and, according to [Legacy Recordings](https://www.legacyrecordings.com/2016/12/07/60-years-since-johnny-mathis-debut-celebrated-essential-2017-releases/), peaked at No. 14 in 1957. The voice in the episode belongs to Kenny James; [Collider](https://collider.com/x-files-episode-home-banned/) reports that Mathis denied the producers his recording.

The song has a source on screen. Morgan told Vitaris he wanted the brothers "driving a big old car" with the tune "playing on the radio," and Manners stages it that way: the white Cadillac starts and the crooner starts with it. The car is not theirs. The deputy reports the next morning that its owner, traced to Baltimore, had run out of gas and left it on the highway. So the brothers ride to a killing in somebody's abandoned Cadillac, to a ballad that charted before Mayberry existed on television.

Manners cuts the drive against a bedtime. Taylor sits on his front step and tells his wife he is "taking one good last look around before it all changes." She says it will still be there in the morning, and they go in. St. James singles out the zoom that lands on the Taylors' door lock, still open, and the tilt up from the car's headlights. The same night, in Mulder's motel room, Scully finds the lock broken, Mulder says nobody needs one around here, and once she has gone he wedges a chair under the knob. His television, which will not pull in the Knicks, settles on a nature program about a pack closing on its prey, the eldest male moving in to make sure of the kill. "We asked, how would animals attack?" Wong said, and his answer, "in packs," is on Mulder's screen while the Cadillac and its song are still on the road.

## Under the bed

Taylor owns a revolver. Earlier that night he took it from a metal box in his desk, looked it over and put it back; Vitaris's summary is that he "considers taking his gun from its locked box, but decides against it." When the Cadillac stops outside he sends his wife under the bed and says he is going for the gun. He is partway down the stairs when the front door, unlocked, swings open. He comes back with a baseball bat. The hour's first daylight scene was a sandlot game, a boy digging in at home plate until his shoe turned up blood and a small hand, so the bat arrives as the last of the town's props. The Peacocks take it away from him.

Manners keeps the scene low, with Barbara Taylor. Vitaris describes "much lifting and descending of the bat," no blow seen landing, over "a relentless series of thwacks." St. James remembers the camera lingering on the wife's fingers as her husband's blood reaches them. Outside the radio is still going. Morgan compared the sequence to the shower in *Psycho* and said the act before it existed so the audience would decide, "I really like this guy." Vitaris places the murder midway through the episode, so the affable lawman is gone with about half the running time left, and the sitcom he was carrying goes with him.

The next morning Deputy Barney, who in Mayberry would have kept his one bullet in a pocket, takes out his gun and proposes three against three. Mulder finds it "a little bit too Chuck Bronson." The deputy opens the Peacocks' front door and an axe on a cord takes his head off. The episode has made a point of four doors by then: the sheriff's office, the motel room, the Taylor house and this one. Mulder's has a chair against it. The Peacocks' has an axe.

## The candlelit speech

At the sandlot Taylor had told the agents he always expected the modern world to find his town: "I knew that day had come." He wanted the killer caught and "the way things are around here" left alone. For most of the hour the brothers only grunt. Then, in a candlelit scene before the raid, one of them talks. "We knew this day was going to happen," he says, that outsiders would "try to change the way things are," and that the family must show them "this is our home." It is nearly the sheriff's speech, phrase for phrase. Morgan said as much to Vitaris: "Thematically, Sheriff Taylor was doing the same thing that the brothers were doing." Standards had asked for silence, and the brothers have so few words that the echo is hard to miss.

The longing for an unchanged town passes from the hero to the lawman to the monster. Mulder opens the hour missing a childhood where you "never had to lock your doors." The Peacock farm, the sheriff says, went up during the Civil War, has no electricity or running water, and its family raise "their own stock." Mrs. Peacock, strapped to a board under her bed, calls that war the War of Northern Aggression. In a back room Mulder picks a newspaper off a stack and reads that Elvis Presley is dead at 42. Asked whether the episode was a comment on family values, Morgan answered, "The Peacocks had family values."

The final scene is the Cadillac idling on an empty road with the radio on. Mrs. Peacock's voice comes from the trunk, promising her eldest "a brand new home." He climbs out, shuts the lid on her and drives. Two days before the broadcast, Fox told Ten Thirteen the hour would carry the series' first parental advisory. "Hey, mom!" Morgan said. "I got a parental advisory!"
