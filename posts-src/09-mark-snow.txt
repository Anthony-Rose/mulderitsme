For thirty to thirty-five minutes of every forty-five minute episode, something was playing under The X-Files. Mark Snow made it alone, on a Synclavier, in a home studio in Santa Monica, and he made it every week for nine seasons. The camera would not show you the monster. The music gave it somewhere to be, and the series leaned on that music harder than on any other part of its craft.

Snow died at his home in Connecticut in July 2025, at 78, and the obituaries led with the theme, the whistle that reached No. 2 on the British singles chart in 1996. The theme is the least typical thing he wrote for the show. The weekly scores were the work, and their texture, machine-made and sourceless, is also the reason the two feature films, scored for orchestra in 1998 and 2008, sound less like The X-Files than any episode does.

## The brief

Chris Carter knew what he did not want. Snow told Sound on Sound in 1997 that the request was for "very supportive, very sustained atmospheric stuff that didn't get in the way," and that Carter was against "obvious melodic, traditional, over-the-top scoring." Snow had spent the 1970s and 1980s writing for orchestras on shows like *Hart to Hart*. The new job asked him to set most of that aside.

The result was closer to sound design than to scoring. He produced between thirty and thirty-five minutes of music for every episode, sometimes forty. There was no orchestra because, as he put it, "there isn't the time to do a live orchestra score with this show." A score took about five to six days, he told Mix in 2000, and some had to be done in three. In the same interview he described his harmonic habits: major seconds "spaced in a very atonal way," and tritones, "a big favorite of mine," with Mahler and late Beethoven somewhere behind them.

Listen to how that plays in an episode. A sustained pad enters under a scene of nothing in particular, an office, a car, a conversation, and does not change for a minute. Then its color shifts, a half step or a filter sweep, before anything on screen has given you a reason. The music is reporting on something the image has not found yet. In a show whose camera agreed to know only what its characters knew, the score was permitted to know more, and it spent the privilege on pressure, low and constant, and almost never on a jolt.

> "I've never been on any other show where the music was that prominent."

Snow said that in his Television Academy Foundation interview, recorded in Santa Monica in March 2016, and he gave Carter the credit. Carter would tell the mixers, he recalled, "the dialogue's good, but I want to hear that music raised." Prominence was the easy part to ask for. The harder part was the sound itself, and here the synthesizers did something an orchestra could not. A string section always sounds like people in a room playing strings; it has an address. A sustained electronic tone has no source. It seems to come from the walls, the ventilation, the ground. On a series about things that might or might not be in the room, music without a source was the right instrument.

## Thirty-five minutes an hour

The quantity matters as much as the sound. Thirty-five minutes of underscore in a forty-five minute drama is a wall-to-wall approach most composers would regard as oppressive, and Carter's own account of the show's fear suggests why it worked here. What scares you most on The X-Files, he told NPR in 2016, "is not what you see," and he finished the thought with "what you hear." A monster that is on screen for ninety seconds across an hour has to exist somewhere for the other fifty-eight minutes. On The X-Files it existed in the score. The drone under the quiet scene was the creature breathing, and when the music stopped, as it did for a basement office conversation or a motel-room joke, the silence read as relief.

Snow told Fandom.com in January 2001 that as the years went on the music became "more musical and less sound design-oriented," and I hear the change in the fourth and fifth seasons, when Scully's illness gave the show a private grief to score and simple piano figures began carrying scenes the dialogue was too reticent for. Even the emotional cues came from the same palette. The piano was sampled, and the warmth sat on top of the same unresolved pads, so the show kept sounding like a machine that had learned to feel.

## The whistle

The main title is the piece everyone can hum and the least representative thing Snow wrote for the show. His first drafts came back from Carter with the words "not quite," and Carter sent him the Smiths' "How Soon Is Now?" for its tremolo guitars, none of which survive in the finished track. The figure that did survive came from an accident. Snow rested his elbow on his keyboard with an echo delay running, liked the rhythm it produced, and built the track on it. He found the melody in a Proteus module preset called "Whistling Joe," and his wife Glynn, hearing it, told him she was a good whistler too. He recorded her, and the finished whistle is her voice blended with the sample, half human and half synthesizer.

The single entered the British chart at the end of March 1996 and sat at No. 2 for three weeks. It ran sixteen weeks in the Top 100, dropped out in July and came back twice in August for a week each time. Snow found the whole thing funny. "Nothing really big happens in the song," he said. "It stays in A-minor, there's no singing, drums or guitars, and it was a smash hit." The hit has shaped his reputation in a way the work does not support. The theme is a tune, and the scores were built to avoid tunes. The Television Academy nominated Snow fifteen times across his career and never gave him the award; six of the nominations were for The X-Files, one for the theme in 1994 and five for the underscore, in 1997, 1998, 1999, 2000 and 2002. The voters, at least, kept nominating the weekly work.

## The orchestra, 1998 and 2008

For the 1998 feature and for *I Want to Believe* a decade later, Snow wrote for orchestra. On the first film he laid down the electronic parts on tape first and played them to the musicians so they could cue against them, a method he described to Mix, and he told Fandom.com, "Ninety percent of the score is big orchestra combined with electronics." The results are fuller and perfectly respectable, and they sound like film scores, which is the problem. Once the X-Files sound has an address, a hall full of players, the dread has a source. The music starts telling you how to feel about what you are watching. On television it had been a report from the part of the room you could not see.

Sean Callery, who considered Snow a mentor, said after his death that the X-Files scores "brought an entirely new language of musical storytelling to television." The language he means is the weekly one: a man alone with a Synclavier, five or six days an episode, thirty-five minutes an hour, for nine years.
