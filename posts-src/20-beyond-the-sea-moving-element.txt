summary: Glen Morgan and James Wong wrote a Scully episode after viewers said she needed humanizing, and David Nutter shot it as two faces in a prison room. Beyond the Sea defines Scully's skepticism as a discipline held against her own grief.
---
The first image in *Beyond the Sea* is an angel on a Christmas tree. Scully's parents are visiting, and the teaser spends its minutes on goodbyes, a father who says less than he means, a daughter who notices. After they leave she looks up to see him sitting across the room in a strange light, his mouth moving without sound. Then the phone rings and her mother tells her he has died of a heart attack. The episode aired on January 7, 1994, the thirteenth hour of the first season, written by Glen Morgan and James Wong and directed by David Nutter, and before the credits it has killed the father of the character the show had spent twelve episodes using as its voice of procedure.

The network balked at the script, and Chris Carter had to back it twice before it got a go-ahead. "They said it was too much like Silence of the Lambs," Morgan told Paula Vitaris in [Cinefantastique's October 1995 X-Files issue](https://archive.org/download/cinefantastique_1970-2002/Cinefantastique%20Vol%2026%20No%206-Vol%2027%20No%201%20%28Oct%201995%29.pdf). The magazine's own caption still called the hour "a Silence of the Lambs riff." Morgan's answer was to build a different man. Lecter was "this kind of cool intellectual," so Boggs became "this manic high-strung cracker." "I was directly trying not to write Hannibal Lecter," he said. The larger difference is in what the killer is selling. Lecter trades clues for Clarice Starling's childhood. Luther Lee Boggs offers Scully a message from her dead father, and the price he names is his own life.

## A book about widows

The episode began with a statistic. Wong told Vitaris about a book Morgan had read which claimed that "75 percent of widows within three months have a vision of their husband." Another was the audience: comments from fans that Scully needed humanizing, which the writers took seriously. "Gillian needed a show to show off her talents," Wong said, and Morgan added that Scully "was doing the same kind of thing too often." Nothing in that brief requires the believer and the skeptic to swap chairs. The script does it anyway, and the mechanism it chooses is the one the book described: a bereaved person sees the dead. Who believes, in this hour, depends on who has just buried someone.

Mulder arrives as the doubter for reasons of his own, since his profile helped put Boggs on death row and he suspects a con. He runs a test, handing Boggs a scrap of cloth without saying where it came from and watching the man perform over a piece of Mulder's own Knicks shirt. Scully arrives fresh from the funeral, and Boggs, as she leaves the room, sings the song from her parents' wedding and calls her Starbuck, her father's name for her. Mulder's line to her, which the magazine printed beside its entry on the hour, is the show's premise with the terms reversed: "Dana, open yourself up to extreme possibilities only when they're the truth." The man who wants to believe is lecturing the scientist on evidence, because this time the evidence is aimed at her.

## Dourif's fee

Morgan and Wong wanted Brad Dourif, an Oscar nominee for *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest*, and the casting nearly failed. According to Eat the Corn's notes on a 2024 [PhileFest panel](https://www.eatthecorn.com/2024/07/05/philefest-the-wongs/) with both writers, Dourif read badly in the room, Wong, who names *Cuckoo's Nest* as a favorite, said the dailies were amazing and that sometimes you have to trust people, Morgan recalled that he was expensive, the two of them offered their own writers' fee to cover him, and Carter phoned Peter Roth at Twentieth Network Television, the Fox studio's TV arm, at Thanksgiving to push it through. Those are a fan site's notes on a panel, not a transcript. Nutter, for his part, told Vitaris that once Dourif arrived his job was to "create a setting where he could be what he really wanted to be," and that he let Dourif "have the stage."

The parents got the same care, unusual for a father who dies in the teaser. Nutter told Vitaris, in a separate piece titled ["Family Ties,"](https://www.eatthecorn.com/itw/1995/10/01/cinefantastique-3/) that Scully's parents needed "real strong qualities and charisma and three dimensions." He had worked with Don Davis before and phoned him for the part; Davis, Major Briggs on *Twin Peaks*, read William Scully as a man "unable to verbalize that love until it was too late." Sheila Larken, whose own father had died of a heart attack the year before, hesitated before taking Margaret, and played the funeral as a woman "so involved with her own pain, she couldn't even react" when her daughter asked whether her father had been proud of her. The mother's answer is that he was her father. The daughter will use the same words at the end of the hour, and they will mean something else.

## The window frame

Nutter described the result to Vitaris in terms most directors would avoid. "In a sense, it was a static episode," he said, and the job was to "let his performance be the moving element." The hour's center is two people in a prison room, the camera holding on faces while Boggs cycles through voices. Vitaris singled out one shot, the door closing behind Anderson and leaving Dourif "centered perfectly in a narrow windowframe." The Vancouver dark that the show usually spent on forests and parking garages is here a prison interior. The hour's subject is a claim, and the way to film a claim is to film the person making it and the person deciding whether to buy.

The script also arranges for Scully to finish alone. Boggs warns Mulder about a white cross and Scully about a blue devil. Mulder ignores his warning and is shot under a wooden cross painted white; Scully heeds hers and lives. With Mulder in a hospital bed, the fourth act is a two-hander by elimination. Scully walks into the interrogation room and threatens Boggs with what she will do if her partner dies, and he invites her to ask for what she came for. She asks to speak to her father. For a few seconds Dourif plays William Scully, then snaps back. "Don't underestimate my fear of dying," he says, and goes on about his terror of the chair. Vitaris wrote that Anderson's Scully "tries mightily to repress both her grief and her belief," and the sentence is accurate about the performance: the repression is the performance.

## The hospital room

Boggs makes a last offer: the message, if Scully attends his execution. She does not go. In the final scene she sits by Mulder's bed and recites the ways the visions could be explained away. Zack Handlen, grading the hour B+ at [The A.V. Club](https://www.avclub.com/the-x-files-beyond-the-sea-gender-bender-lazuru-1798204707) in 2008, called that scene the worst part of the episode. He wished it "didn't make her look quite so weak" and felt her admission that she was afraid to believe "stacks the cards too heavily in Mulder's favor." Meghan Deans, rewatching for [Tor.com](https://reactormag.com/reopening-the-x-files-qbeyond-the-seaq/), now Reactor, in 2011, read the scene with more sympathy and suggested that Scully's fear of believing and her fear of death might be a single fear.

Handlen's objection treats the scene as a scorecard. It works better as a definition of what Scully's skepticism is made of. Her doubt, as the hour defines it, is a discipline she practices on herself when she wants something to be true, and the hour shows what she does with the impossible once she has seen it: she sorts it, names the alternatives, and declines the offer. When Mulder asks how she can know what her father meant to tell her, her answer is that he was her father. She has arrived at a belief with no evidence behind it, a belief about a person, and the hour lets her keep that one. Hulu's series, confirmed so far only as two FBI agents assigned to a long-closed division, has yet to say what divides its pair.

Davis came back in *One Breath* the following season to deliver the message Scully had refused to collect, and Morgan told Vitaris how that episode came about. The writers were in Vancouver during the shooting of *Fire* when Duchovny kept circling them. He told them he liked *Beyond the Sea*. Morgan replied, "Thanks a lot, Gillian did good." Duchovny asked, "When are you guys going to write me one?" Morgan and Wong had hoped *Little Green Men* would be his *Beyond the Sea*; by Vitaris's account it fell short, and *One Breath* was the one. Duchovny telephoned them with a note the script did not contain: "I think Mulder should cry." He did it in one take.
