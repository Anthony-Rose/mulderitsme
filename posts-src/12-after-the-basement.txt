summary: In January 2007 the producers of Lost asked ABC for an end date and cited The X-Files as the reason. Vince Gilligan built Breaking Bad to be finite. The writers who came out of the X-Files room went on to make shows that could stop, and that is the part of the show's legacy least often counted.
---
On January 14, 2007, at the Television Critics Association press tour in Pasadena, the producers of *Lost* were asked about their show's future, and Carlton Cuse reached for The X-Files. "That was a great show that probably ran two seasons too long," he said. "That is a cautionary tale for us." He and Damon Lindelof said they were in discussions with ABC about picking an end point. That May the network agreed to a 2010 finish, and *Lost* played its last three seasons toward a date fixed in advance.

The commercial logic of American network television had always been that a hit runs until it stops being one. *Lost* argued for the right to stop while it was still a hit, and the argument it made was The X-Files. The show that had reinvented the serialized mystery was now the example its heirs cited when asking to be allowed to end. Influence of the imitative kind is easy to find: the two-track design of weekly cases over a long arc, the dark frame that network drama adopted as a default, the alumni list. All of that is real. Cuse was describing a second kind, the influence a show has on people who watched it fail at something, in public, for years, and who then went off to run the medium.

## Gilligan and Drive

Vince Gilligan wrote some of the show's best standalone hours, *Pusher*, *Bad Blood*, *Drive*, and in his Television Academy Foundation interview he says it was *Drive*, in 1998, where he first worked with Bryan Cranston and realized the actor could play what he calls a "sympathetic creep." A decade later he built Walter White on that discovery.

The casting anecdote hides a structural one. *Breaking Bad* has no mythology, no hidden conspiracy that advances a few episodes a season and defers its answers to May. It is one story about one man, and Gilligan told TheWrap in 2014 that it "was designed to be a finite, ultimate, close-ended story." Everything he took from The X-Files was the standalone's discipline: a contained situation, a guest actor given room to be human, a moral pressure that resolves inside the hour. Everything he declined was the long game. One of the most acclaimed dramas of the following decade came from an X-Files writer who kept the show's episodes and left its architecture behind.

## The room's graduates

The writers' room did become a kind of academy. Howard Gordon went on to *24* and co-created *Homeland*, sharing the 2012 Emmy for its pilot script with Alex Gansa and Gideon Raff. Frank Spotnitz, who wrote forty-four episodes, more than anyone but Carter, took *The Man in the High Castle* to Amazon after Scott Free had developed it for the BBC and passed, and Syfy had passed in turn. Glen Morgan and James Wong rewrote a spec script that Jeffrey Reddick had first pitched in 1994 as an X-Files episode called "Flight 180"; Wong directed it as *Final Destination* in 2000 and it became a franchise. David Nutter, a mainstay of the early seasons from *Ice* to *Clyde Bruckman's Final Repose*, became the industry's pilot specialist. By the count on his Michael J. Fox Foundation biography, twenty-one of his twenty-four pilots went to series, among them *Smallville*, *Supernatural*, *Arrow* and *The Flash*.

Read as a set of structural choices, the list has a shape. *24* is built on a clock, a constraint that forces resolution by design. *Homeland* reinvents itself season to season. *The Man in the High Castle* is a closed alternate world with a novel's shape. *Final Destination* is a rules machine in which the only mystery is who dies next. None of them, with one exception, built a show on an open-ended conspiracy that promises a truth and keeps the promise in escrow. They had watched the parent show try.

## Supernatural, 2005 to 2020

The exception is *Supernatural*, and it paid the full price. Kim Manners, who directed more X-Files episodes than anyone, signed on in 2005 to direct and produce it and worked on it until his death in January 2009. The series copied the model more faithfully than the others, two investigators in a car and a monster a week, with a demonic mythology running underneath. It premiered in 2005 and ended on November 19, 2020, after fifteen seasons, by which point it had staged an apocalypse, survived it, and kept going for a decade. Entertainment Tonight's roundup of the finale found a fan base "divided" and a response made of "outrage and uncontrollable weeping," from people who had stayed through years of diminishing returns. Anyone who sat through the last seasons of The X-Files knew the feeling. The one descendant that adopted the engine whole ran it for fifteen years and ended to the same noise.

## Millennium and The Lone Gunmen

Carter tested the lesson himself before anyone else did. *Millennium*, which premiered on Fox on October 25, 1996, gave Lance Henriksen's Frank Black a darker world than Mulder's and a secret society, the Millennium Group, that Glen Morgan and James Wong rebuilt when they took over the second season and that changed again when Carter took the show back for its third. It lasted three seasons. *The Lone Gunmen*, the spinoff about Mulder's hacker allies, premiered on March 4, 2001 with a pilot, credited to Carter, Gilligan, John Shiban and Spotnitz, in which conspirators try to fly a passenger jet by remote control into the World Trade Center; the plane pulls up at the last second. Six months later the episode became an unsettling footnote in the franchise, and Spotnitz told TV Guide the following June that he had assumed nobody noticed. "I guess so few people saw the show," he said. It had been cancelled after thirteen episodes.

Neither spinoff ran long enough to face the problem the parent show faced, but both show Carter reaching for the same device, a secret organization whose meaning is deferred, and both suggest he understood its appeal better than its cost. When Fox brought The X-Files back in 2016 and 2018, the revival reopened the conspiracy twice more and closed it twice more. The series Hulu ordered from Ryan Coogler in October 2026 is reported to start from two new agents, played by Danielle Deadwyler and Himesh Patel, and not from Mulder and Scully. If that holds, the franchise will have drawn the conclusion its imitators drew in 2007.

## The deliberate gifts

The show also gave the medium things it meant to give. The hybrid of case and arc is now the default shape of genre television, and The X-Files proved that a network audience would follow both tracks at once. The 35mm darkness became the look of serious drama once the screens caught up. The guest-star economy, in which a Bryan Cranston or a Peter Boyle, who won an Emmy for *Clyde Bruckman's Final Repose*, could be handed an hour and trusted to carry it, is where a generation of character actors and the writers who loved them learned their trade.

A legacy is measured by what people do with it, and the people who came out of that room built shows that could end. They kept the episode and gave up the long promise, put dates on their finales, and cited the show by name when they asked their networks for the right to stop. *Lost* got its date in May 2007 and kept it. The finale aired on May 23, 2010, three years and four months after Cuse stood in Pasadena and called The X-Files a cautionary tale.
