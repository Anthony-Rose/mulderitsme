title: The Case Against Mulder: I Want to Believe
date: 2026-10-16
tag: The Films
hue: 205
summary: The second X-Files film opened fourth, a week behind The Dark Knight, and the calendar has carried the blame ever since. The film deserves a harder look: it is the X-Files story that puts Mulder's faith on trial and rules for the skeptic.
---
*The X-Files: I Want to Believe* opened on July 25, 2008, one week after *The Dark Knight*, finished fourth behind *Step Brothers* and *Mamma Mia!* with just over $10 million, and ended with about $69 million worldwide against a $30 million budget. Chris Carter has said that Fox wanted it made for "roughly a third" of what *Fight the Future* cost (the published figures, $30 million against $66 million, make it closer to half) and that the studio planned to rely on word of mouth from fans and skip the advertising. All of that is true. The failure worth talking about is that Carter and Frank Spotnitz wrote the one X-Files story in which Mulder is the problem, and released it to an audience that had spent fifteen years being told he was the point.

Seen as Scully's film, it coheres. Seen as Mulder's, the way the title and the poster invite, it falls apart, because the plot keeps refusing to pay him, and I think the refusal is deliberate. Carter's own 2026 recut, which strips back the romance to push the horror, confirms that the theatrical version was not the film he wanted either. The 2008 release is an X-Files movie that turned out to be about its skeptic, and it is better for it.

## Where the film finds him

The series spent nine seasons teaching the audience to read Mulder's isolation as integrity. The film opens by reading it as damage. He is in hiding, discredited by the Bureau, in a room papered with clippings, and the first thing the story asks of him is whether he will come back to the work at all. The agents who fetch him, Amanda Peet's Dakota Whitney and Xzibit's Mosley Drummy, are a procedural audience, one willing to believe and one not, and the film gives neither the satisfaction of being right.

They bring him Father Joseph Crissman, Billy Connolly's defrocked priest, a man who molested thirty-seven altar boys and now lives in a dormitory of offenders and reports visions of a kidnapped agent. The show's method had been to put a believer and a skeptic in a car and let the case referee their argument. Here the case is a pedophile who may be a prophet, and the film asks Mulder to want him to be real. Mulder does, and the film lets you watch belief, which the series had treated as courage, behave like an appetite that takes its evidence from anyone.

Connolly plays it without charm, and critics split on him. Peter Rainer in the Christian Science Monitor wrote that he "steals the acting honors"; Den of Geek's Simon Brew wrote, "Bluntly, this reviewer never bought him." Both reactions answer the same choice. Connolly refuses to make Father Joe likable or theatrical, so the audience gets no permission to enjoy him and no permission to dismiss him. His visions find the missing agent's remains in the snow. They never find her alive. The gift is real and it is useless, and I cannot think of a colder thing the franchise ever said about what Mulder wants.

## Scully's film

Meanwhile the movie's argument is happening in a hospital. Scully is a physician at a Catholic institution, treating a boy named Christian whose symptoms point to Sandhoff disease, a terminal condition, and arguing for a painful experimental stem cell treatment that Father Ybarra, a priest on the hospital's staff, wants to stop in favor of letting the child die gently. The believer in the room is the Church, and it counsels resignation. The skeptic insists on a treatment that probably will not work. Scully's belief costs her something, because a patient is attached to it, while Mulder's is pinned to a corkboard in a back room.

The film cuts between the two tracks with a bluntness Brew put down to small-screen habits; Carter's style, he wrote, "is predominantly to serve a smaller screen, which accounts for the amount of time we spend in close-ups." He has a point about the close-ups, but the intercutting is where the film makes its case. Every time Mulder gets closer to the kidnappers, Scully gets closer to a decision about a child, and the film weights the second. When Father Joe tells her, "Don't give up," the line is written so that it can only apply to the boy. It never belongs to Mulder's case. What grace the film has goes to the hospital.

Scully's own line is the film's verdict, and it contradicts the poster. "This isn't my life anymore, Mulder," she tells him. "I'm done chasing monsters in the dark." The series had let her say versions of this for years and always brought her back to the car. The film takes her at her word. It lets her be done, and then shows what Mulder looks like without her: a man in snow, following the visions of a sex offender toward an operating table.

## Snow instead of dark

Bill Roe, who had shot the series from its move to Los Angeles through the final season, photographs the Vancouver winter as the show's darkness turned inside out. Carter has said he went through nine pairs of shoes trying to keep his feet warm, and the cold is in the frame: wide fields of white in which nothing can hide and figures stand out like marks on paper. The series' signature image was a flashlight beam picking out what you were allowed to see. Here you can see everything, and it does not help. A severed arm turns up in a snowfield. The kidnappers' surgical room is lit like a clinic. Even the horror the plot is building toward, a transplant that keeps a head alive in ice while it waits for a body, is shown with the lights on.

The inversion was deliberate, and the audience punished it. The film's few gestures toward the old visual vocabulary, the night scenes near the kidnappers' compound, are the moments that play most like the show. The rest reads as flat, and was meant to. The show's aesthetic argued that the truth is hidden and can be found. The film's argues that the truth is in the daylight and is awful: a sick child, a dying man, a priest who did what he did. There is nothing in the dark to chase, only what you decide to do about what you can already see.

## The 2026 recut

Carter has said the Fox executives who saw his first cut used the words "torture porn," that the ratings board told them "This isn't a PG-13 movie," and that the version released was shaped by cutting the horror back and leaning on the Mulder and Scully relationship, which he now says he had "shoehorned in there to please the original executives." His R-rated recut, which reached Hulu in August 2026 under the subtitle *Vrach Frankenshteyn* and runs about 98 minutes against the original 104, undoes that trade. It restores the darkness and takes away some of the romance, of which, he says, the revival seasons had given everyone their fill.

By Carter's account the recut changes the horror and the romance, which leaves the hospital, the boy, the priest and Scully's refusal where they were. The romance could come or go and the gore could be dialed up or down, but the film's spine was always a woman choosing a patient over a partner's obsession. Carter calls the theatrical version "a Mulder and Scully relationship movie that happened to have an element of an X-File in it." I would call it a film about one half of the relationship winning an argument the series had rigged for fifteen years, and the audience, reasonably enough, did not come to a summer movie to watch Mulder lose.

Seen that way, the fourth-place opening says something about what the franchise's audience would and would not buy. A conspiracy with aliens would have sold better. So would a Mulder who was right. The film offered a question instead, one the show had spent its life avoiding: what if the person who wants to believe is the one who needs to stop? Franchise films rarely ask that. Eighteen years later Carter went back into this one and restored the horror he had been made to cut, and the title he gave the new version, *Vrach Frankenshteyn*, is Russian for Doctor Frankenstein, the man in the operating room with the ice.
