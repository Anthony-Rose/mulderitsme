# Mulder, It's Me

An unofficial X-Files film journal served at https://mulderitsme.net. See `README.md` for layout, the essay format and the build step (`python build_blog.py`).

## Authorship rule

Only Claude Fable writes the actual prose on this site. This covers every word readers see: the essays in `posts-src/*.txt`, the homepage copy in `index.html` (hero, visual grammar, filmmakers, syllabus, lineage, footer), `404.html`, and any new page text.

If you are not Fable, do not edit site copy yourself. Delegate the writing or editing pass to a Fable subagent and let it make the prose changes. You may still do everything around the writing: research, fact-checking, sourcing, running the build, and committing.

The prose must sound as authentically human as possible. Keep the confident, literate film-journal voice, but avoid the tells of machine writing: the "it is not X, it is Y" antithesis used on a loop, formula openers repeated across pieces ("The received account goes like this", "Start with", "Notice what that does"), over-balanced triads, and closing sentences that restate the thesis with a neat button. Vary sentence length, allow plain sentences, and trim rather than pad.

## House style

No em dashes or en dashes anywhere. Every fact verified, with sources listed at the end of each essay. Keep the markup and header format described in `README.md` intact.

### Essay rules

These eight rules come from the November 2026 style review of all 19 essays. They apply to every essay, including drafts from the twice-weekly routine.

#### Openings

Open on the thing itself: a shot, a date, a quote, a scene, a production fact. Do not open by stating the consensus you are about to overturn. Banned openers: "The standard story", "The usual reading", "The usual way to describe", "The received account", "The explanation everyone settled on", "is remembered as", "is usually described as", "has been treated ever since as", "Ask a longtime viewer", "Ask an X-Files viewer", "Fans have spent thirty years", "the hour everyone calls". If the received view has to be named, name it in the second or third paragraph, in one sentence, with a source who holds it. Do not follow it with "I think that account is wrong" or "I want to argue the reverse"; make the argument.

#### Contrast

Reversal is this journal's method, so the sentence shapes that express it wear out fastest. Per essay, allow at most two of any of these: "was never X. It was Y", "X, not Y" as a sentence or appositive, "not X so much as Y", "rather than", "the opposite", "inverted", "inside out", "backward", "the reverse". Where the contrast is the thesis, state the positive half and let the evidence carry the negative half. The essay titles and deks follow the same rule: no "The [noun] Is the [noun]" title more than once in ten essays, and no dek built as "X is remembered/praised/credited for A. Its real B was C."

#### Verdict sentences

A verdict sentence names what the preceding fact means: "That is the film's quiet indictment", "The point is the substitution", "The name is the tell", "That is the episode in miniature", "That is Bartley's method run backward", "The reason sits in the paint." Allow at most two per essay and never two in one paragraph. Prefer to let the fact stand; if the meaning needs stating, attach it to the fact in the same sentence rather than giving it a sentence of its own. Banned: "the tell" as a noun, "in miniature", "the point is", "That is true and it is not very interesting", "the interesting failure", "says more than he probably meant to", "too neat to be an accident".

#### Endings

A paragraph may not end on a punchline more than three times in an essay (a punchline here is a closing sentence of seven words or fewer that restates the paragraph). The final paragraph may not restate the thesis or the title. End on something the reader has not had yet: a quote, a date, a frame, a credit, a fact. Do not end on an aphorism about the whole show ("One of those habits will give", "It worked on him", "the budget is what is wrong with it").

#### Headings

No heading may begin with "What" followed by "actually". "Actually" does not appear in headings at all. At most one "Noun, and qualifier" heading per essay ("The theme, and its accident"). Headings name the section's subject (a scene, a person, a date), not the section's conclusion.

#### Adverbs, intensifiers, superlatives

One use each per essay, at most: exactly, precisely, actually, really, simply, quietly, plainly, literally, finally. "The whole", "the only", "the one thing", "the clearest", and "the most X the show ever Y" are claims; each needs the sentence that proves it or gets cut. "Almost", "nearly", "barely", "mostly" are fine; they are hedges the argument needs.

#### Reader stage directions and lists

At most one imperative addressed to the reader per essay ("Watch", "Look at", "Consider", "Listen to", "Put X beside Y", "Picture", "Strip the logline", "Set that beside"). Colon-led lists of three ("three principles: motivated lighting, an active camera, and restrained color") at most two per essay; where the source gives three, quote the source. Pairs and fours are fine.

#### Vocabulary, self-reference, attribution

Rotate the house nouns: "grammar", "withhold" (and withheld, withholding), "instrument", "engine", "machine", "structural", "account", "testimony", "the record" each appear at most twice per essay. Refer to another essay on the site at most once per piece, by its title in a link, never as "the first essay on this site" or "elsewhere on this site". A pronoun may not open a section or a paragraph that follows a heading; name the person. When a claim rests on fan notes, a blog, or an anonymous source, say so in the sentence that makes the claim, not only in the Sources list. Inline links go on the first mention of a source and nowhere else; the Sources list carries the full citation. Do not mix an essay that links nothing inline with one that links every paragraph.
