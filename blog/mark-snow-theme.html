CASE FILE 12 // MUSIC

Four Notes and a Whistle: The Sound of The X-Files

Filed October 9, 20262 min readBy the Basement Desk
DECLASSIFIED

Mark Snow's music did as much to make the show scary as anything on screen. Here's how one strange theme became a pop hit.

You can probably hear it right now. A cold, echoing whistle over a slow electronic pulse. The X-Files theme is one of the most recognizable pieces of TV music ever written, and it came from a composer working mostly alone with a room full of synthesizers.

Mark Snow

Mark Snow scored every episode of the original series and both films, and he came back for the revival. He worked fast, often composing a full episode score in a matter of days, and almost entirely with electronic instruments. That gave the show a sound that was cheaper than an orchestra and, as it turned out, far creepier.

The theme

The story Snow has told over the years is that the theme came together only after several rejected attempts. Chris Carter wanted something different from the usual TV opening, and Snow found the key sound while experimenting with an echo effect on his keyboard. The whistle melody with the long, repeating echo was the result. Whatever the exact details, it sounded like nothing else on TV in 1993.

A surprise hit

In 1996 the theme was released as a single and became a hit in several countries, including the United Kingdom, where it climbed high in the charts. Very few TV themes have done that, and it happened mostly because people just wanted to hear it again.

The score underneath

The theme gets the attention, but the episode scores are just as important. Snow's music fills in what the low budget couldn't show. A slow drone tells you something is wrong before anything happens on screen. Long, cold pads make a parking lot feel like an alien landscape. In the quieter character episodes, he switches to soft piano that's often more affecting than the dialogue.

Listen closely to "Paper Clip," "Memento Mori," or "Field Trip" and you can hear how much work the music is doing.

Why it still works

Most TV music from the early 90s sounds dated. Snow's work mostly doesn't, because it was never trying to sound like the music of its time. It was trying to sound like the dark between the trees. Put it on in a dark room tonight and see how long you last.
