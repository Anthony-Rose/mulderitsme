Lighting the Unseen: The Flashlight Aesthetic

Network television in 1993 was built to be seen. The X-Files was built to withhold. How a weekly drama learned to treat darkness as its primary material.

October 16, 20263 min read790 words

Turn on a network drama from the early 1990s and the first thing you notice is how much you can see. Sets are evenly lit from above, faces are filled from every side, and the image is tuned for a small, dim cathode-ray screen in a bright living room. Clarity was the job. Nobody wanted a viewer squinting at a mid-size Zenith to work out who was talking.

The X-Files arrived in September 1993 and quietly refused that job. It was shot on 35mm film, much of it at night, and it let large parts of the frame fall into true black. That choice wasn't only an aesthetic preference. It solved the central problem of a show about things that can't be proved: how do you keep showing an audience something you aren't allowed to show them?

Black as a working color

The show's answer was to make darkness active. In most television, shadow is what's left over after the important things are lit. Here it is the thing being composed. A doorway at the far end of a hallway, a tree line at the edge of a field, the gap behind a furnace: those are the areas the camera keeps returning to, and they are almost always unlit.

That has a practical benefit on a television budget. A creature that is never fully seen never has to look convincing in full light. But the bigger effect is psychological. When the frame is mostly black, the viewer does the work of filling it, and what a viewer imagines is nearly always worse than what a makeup department can build in eight days.

The flashlight as point of view

The signature image of the series is two agents moving through a dark space with flashlights raised. It became shorthand for the show so quickly that it's easy to forget how unusual it was as a lighting strategy.

A handheld flashlight is a terrible key light by conventional standards. It is small, hard, and moves constantly. On this show it does three jobs at once. It lights the scene. It tells you where Mulder and Scully are looking. And it defines the edge of what is known, because anything outside the beam is, for the length of that shot, unknowable. The lighting design and the point-of-view design are the same thing.

That's also why the beams cross so often. Two agents with two flashlights is a visual argument between two people who are looking at the same room and seeing different things.

Haze, backlight and silhouette

Watch the early seasons closely and you'll see smoke in the air in scenes where nothing is burning. Atmospheric haze turns every light source into a visible shaft. A window becomes a wall of light, a car's headlights become solid beams, and the flashlights carve visible cones through the room.

The show paired that haze with hard backlight. Figures are often rimmed from behind and left with little or no light on the front, so they read first as silhouettes and only later as people. A man standing in a doorway could be an ally, a suspect or something worse, and for a few seconds the image won't tell you which.

The people who shot it

The look was established on the pilot by Thomas Del Ruth, whose photography earned a nomination from the American Society of Cinematographers. John S. Bartley then shaped the Vancouver years. His work drew ASC nominations for Duane Barry, 731 and Grotesque, and he won the Emmy for Grotesque in 1996 after a nomination for One Breath the year before. Joel Ransom shot episodes in season five, including the black-and-white Post-Modern Prometheus, which brought him an Emmy nomination of his own.

It's rare for television cinematographers to be recognized this consistently, and rarer still for a show to be identified so strongly by its photography. Plenty of viewers who couldn't name a single person who worked behind the camera could still describe what an X-File looked like.

Why it mattered

The darkness of The X-Files fed directly into the look of television that followed. Prestige dramas of the 2000s and 2010s routinely let faces fall into shadow, trusted audiences to read a frame that wasn't fully lit, and treated night exteriors as a mood rather than a problem. By then the screens had changed: flat panels with real contrast made those images easier to read than the old tubes ever did.

What makes the original achievement more impressive is that the show did it before the screens were ready. It was composing for a medium that, technically, couldn't fully show it what it was doing. It trusted the audience to lean in. Most of them did.
