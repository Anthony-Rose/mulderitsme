Essay No. 01 · Cinematography

The Flashlight Is the Camera

The flashlight on The X-Files is usually described as a lighting style. It was something stranger: the show's only honest narrator, and the device that turned a network procedural into a first-person film.

October 16, 20265 min read1,226 words

The standard story about how The X-Files looked is a budget story. A young show with no money shot at night in Vancouver, discovered that darkness was cheaper than sets, and made a virtue of it. None of that is wrong, and the people who made the show have said as much. John S. Bartley, who photographed the first three seasons and died in Los Angeles in August 2025 at 78, told the ASC that "we didn't have any money, and that had a lot to do with the look." But budget explains why the frame was dark. It does not explain the thing that actually defined the series, which is what the show put into that darkness: a handheld beam, carried by a character, pointed where the character chose to point it.

The flashlight was never a lighting instrument in the ordinary sense. It was a narrative one. In a series where every other source of information lies, where witnesses contradict themselves, government men dissemble and the agents' own memories are tampered with, the beam is the one thing on screen that cannot be made to lie. What it touches is real. What it does not touch is unknown. That makes The X-Files, for most of its running time, a first-person film that only pretends to be shot in the third person.

Hard light, no fill

Bartley shot at T2.8 even in daylight interiors and, rather than adding light, pushed the latitude of Kodak's EXR stocks, which he said had let him go as much as five stops under. "I don't use much fill," he told Kodak's In Camera in 1995, and he meant it: editors sometimes told him they could not see what was happening on their Avid monitors. The show was framed in Super 35 because Fox wanted a wide negative for future high-definition syndication, which is why the series could be reframed for widescreen two decades later. The actors carried xenon flashlights, and in the darkest sets, the arctic submarine of End Game among them, those beams were the only source. The gaffers kicked them back with Rosco pebble bounce so that the light returned onto the actors' faces, which is how a character holding a flashlight ends up lit by her own flashlight.

That last detail matters more than it sounds. In conventional television the key light comes from somewhere the character cannot see: a lamp on a stand, a soft box above the lens. On The X-Files the key light is in the character's hand. Where the actor looks, the light goes, and where the light goes, the audience is permitted to see. Point of view and exposure become the same decision.

"You light the walls, but you don't light the actors."

A still photographer said that to Bartley on set, claiming to have figured him out, and Bartley told Kodak in 2011 that sometimes it was true. Walls, doorways and the far ends of corridors were lit; the agents were allowed to find their light at the right moment or not at all. The show's famous silhouettes, a figure rimmed from behind in a doorway with nothing on the face, are the direct consequence. A person is only identified when a flashlight finds them. Until then they are a shape.

The beam as witness

Consider how this plays out across the first season. In Squeeze and Tooms, the threat lives in ventilation shafts and crawlspaces, and the camera does not go in after it. The flashlight does. We learn the geography of Eugene Tooms's world a cone at a time, and the horror is that the cone is small. In Ice, which Bartley singled out as the episode that took the series "to the next level," a research station becomes a sealed box in which every character is a suspect, and the lighting stops distinguishing between the people the agents trust and the people they do not. In Darkness Falls, the premise is the aesthetic made literal: the thing in the forest can only move in the dark, and the characters survive exactly as long as a generator and a few bulbs hold out. The show had been treating light as the boundary of knowledge for a year. Here it wrote an episode in which light is the boundary of life.

What the flashlight is not doing in these scenes is creating mood, or not primarily. Mood is the smoke and the backlight, the steam Bartley pumped into exteriors so that every source became a visible shaft. The flashlight is doing epistemology. It tells you what Mulder has established and what Scully has established, and when the two beams cross in a room, it stages their disagreement as a composition. Two cones of light sweeping the same space and settling on different objects is the show's argument in a single frame.

This is why the device survived every change of personnel. Bartley left after season three; Joel Ransom, promoted from camera operator midway through the fourth season after Jon Joffin's brief tenure, carried the look through the end of the Vancouver years and earned an Emmy nomination for the black-and-white Post-Modern Prometheus; Bill Roe inherited it in Los Angeles. Directors came and went. Pacing, humor and the color palette all changed. The flashlight did not, because it was not a style that belonged to a cinematographer. It was a rule of the fiction.

What the camera refuses

The usual way to describe the show's visual ethic is that it withheld. It did, and Bartley's own account is the plainest statement of the method: "We didn't reveal what's in the darkness." But withholding is a negative description, and it misses the positive structure the flashlight supplies. A monster movie that simply keeps its monster in shadow is cheating the audience. A monster movie in which the audience sees exactly what the protagonist's flashlight sees, no more and no less, is playing fair. The restriction is motivated. Every unseen thing is unseen for a reason that exists inside the story.

That is the difference between The X-Files and the prestige dramas that later borrowed its palette. Those shows are dark because darkness reads as serious. The X-Files was dark because its characters were carrying their own light into places where nobody else had been, and the camera was honest enough to admit it could not see any further than they could.

The record

The approach was recognized at the time, which is rarer in television cinematography than it should be. Thomas Del Ruth's pilot brought him an ASC nomination in the pilots and movies category in 1994. Bartley was Emmy-nominated in 1995 and won in 1996 for Grotesque, an episode that is almost entirely flashlights moving through an artist's studio full of gargoyles; the ASC nominated him three years running, 1995 through 1997. Ransom's Emmy nomination followed in 1998. Three cinematographers honored on one network series inside five years, before Roe added two ASC awards of his own in Los Angeles.

Chris Carter's brief, as the ASC recorded it, was that the show should be "dark, moody, mysterious and sometimes claustrophobic." Any competent crew could have delivered that. What the Vancouver unit delivered instead was a camera that agreed to know only what its characters knew, and a prop that made the agreement visible every week.
