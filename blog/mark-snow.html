The Score Was the Monster

Mark Snow's music for The X-Files is remembered for a whistled theme. His real achievement was a weekly score that occupied the darkness the camera refused to show, which is why the two feature films, scored for orchestra, sound less like the show than any episode.

October 16, 20265 min read1,209 words

The X-Files is remembered as a show that would not let you see the monster. That is only half true. The camera would not let you see it. The score let you hear it, for thirty minutes an hour, every week, for nine seasons. Mark Snow's music did more than accompany the show's withheld images. It filled the space those images left empty, and the series depended on it more completely than on any other single element of its craft.

That is a stronger claim than the usual praise for Snow, who died at his home in Connecticut in July 2025 at 78, and it has a consequence that admirers of the show may resist. When the franchise moved to the big screen in 1998 and 2008, the music went orchestral. Both scores are accomplished. Neither sounds like The X-Files, because the thing that made the television music work was not Snow's melodic gift but the specific, machine-made texture he produced alone in a room on the west side of Los Angeles, and that texture did not survive the hiring of a string section.

The brief

Chris Carter knew what he did not want. Snow told Sound on Sound in 1997 that the request was for "very supportive, very sustained atmospheric stuff that didn't get in the way," and that Carter was explicitly against "obvious melodic, traditional, over-the-top scoring." For a composer who had spent the 1970s and 1980s writing for full orchestras on shows like Hart to Hart, this was a license to abandon almost everything the job had previously meant.

What he built instead was closer to sound design than to scoring. Working on a Synclavier in his home studio in Santa Monica, he produced between thirty and thirty-five minutes of music for every forty-five minute episode, sometimes forty. There was no orchestra because, as he put it, "there isn't the time to do a live orchestra score with this show." Each score took about five to six days, he told Mix in 2000, and some were done in three. In the same interview he described his harmonic habits as major seconds spaced atonally and tritones, the intervals that refuse to resolve, with Mahler and late Beethoven behind them.

Listen to how that plays in an episode. A sustained pad enters under a scene of nothing in particular, an office, a car, a conversation, and does not change for a minute. Then its color shifts, slightly, a half step or a filter sweep, before anything on screen has given you a reason. The music is not reacting to the image. It is reporting on something the image has not found yet. In a show whose camera agreed to know only what its characters knew, the score was the one voice permitted to know more, and it used that privilege sparingly, as a low, constant pressure rather than a jump scare.

"The spectrum of sound really warmed up and started to breathe."

Snow said that in a 2016 Television Academy interview about his switch to all-electronic scoring in the late 1980s, and the sentence explains why synthesizers suited this show in a way an orchestra could not. A string section always sounds like people in a room playing strings; it has an address. A sustained electronic tone has no source. It seems to come from the walls, the ventilation, the ground. On a series about things that might or might not be in the room, music without a source is the correct instrument.

Volume as structure

The sheer quantity matters as much as the sound. Thirty-five minutes of underscore in a forty-five minute drama is a wall-to-wall approach most composers would regard as oppressive, and Carter's own account of the show's fear suggests why it worked. The scares, he told NPR in 2016, come from "what you hear." A monster that is on screen for ninety seconds across an hour has to exist somewhere for the other fifty-eight minutes. On The X-Files it existed in the score. The drone under the quiet scene was the creature breathing. When the music finally stopped, as it occasionally did for a basement office conversation or a motel-room joke, the silence was the relief.

Snow told Fandom.com in 2001 that as the years went on the music became "more musical and less sound design-oriented," and that is audible in the fourth and fifth seasons, when Scully's illness gave the show a sustained private grief to score. Simple piano figures began to carry scenes that the dialogue was too reticent to carry. But even the emotional cues were built from the same palette. The piano was sampled; the warmth sat on top of the same unresolved pads. The show never stopped sounding like a machine that had learned to feel.

The theme, and its accident

The main title is the one piece of Snow's X-Files music that everyone can hum, and it is the least representative. Snow's first drafts came back from Carter with the verdict "not quite," and Carter sent him the Smiths' "How Soon Is Now?" for the tremolo guitars. The breakthrough was a mistake. Snow rested his elbow on his keyboard with an echo delay running, liked the rhythmic figure it produced, and built the track on it. He tried violin, flute and piano samples for the melody before settling on a Proteus module preset called "Whistling Joe," then layered in his wife Glynn, who pointed out she was a good whistler too. The finished sound is half human, half machine.

Released as a single in Britain in March 1996, it climbed to No. 2 on the Official Singles Chart, ran sixteen consecutive weeks in the Top 100 and returned twice more that summer. Snow found this funny. "Nothing really big happens in the song," he said at the time; it stays in A minor, with no singing, no drums, no guitars. But the theme's popularity has distorted his reputation. It is a tune, and the scores were deliberately not tunes. The Television Academy nominated Snow fifteen times over his career and never gave him the award; six of those nominations were for The X-Files, one for the theme in 1994 and five for the underscore between 1997 and 2002. The voters, to their credit, recognized that the weekly work was the work.

What the films lost

Which brings us back to the movies. For the 1998 feature and I Want to Believe a decade later, Snow wrote for orchestra; on the first film he recorded his electronics first and laid the players over them. The results are fuller, more conventionally cinematic, and perfectly respectable. They also prove the point. Give the X-Files sound an address, a hall full of musicians, and the dread acquires a source. It becomes a film score, telling you how to feel about what you are watching, rather than a report from the part of the room you cannot see.

Composer Sean Callery, who considered Snow a mentor, said his X-Files scores "brought an entirely new language of musical storytelling to television." That language had little to do with the whistle. It was a man alone with a Synclavier, filling thirty-five minutes an hour with sounds that had no origin.
