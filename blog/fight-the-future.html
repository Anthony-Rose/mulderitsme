Fight the Future Is Best When It Forgets It Is a Movie

The 1998 film spent its money on a spaceship under the ice and a helicopter over a cornfield. Its best scenes cost almost nothing, and they are the ones that still look like The X-Files.

October 16, 20266 min read1,316 words

The backhanded compliment has followed this film since 1998, and Salon's Jeff Stark put it most plainly when the DVD arrived in 2001: the picture "comes off like an episode of the television show with a really big budget," which, he allowed, was not a terrible thing. It was meant as a limitation. I think it names the film's only real virtue, and that the film is weakest at exactly the moments it tries hardest to disprove the charge.

The X-Files, released June 19, 1998 and later known by the subtitle Fight the Future, is a good two-hour episode with a bad blockbuster grafted onto it. Every sequence that enlarges the series, the Antarctic ice station, the craft beneath it, the helicopter chase through the corn, is where the picture goes slack and generic. Every sequence that behaves like television, a hallway, a bar, an alley, a federal building with a bomb in a vending machine, is where it is alive. The film was conceived to prove the show could scale. What it proved was that the show's grammar did not want to.

Built to change nothing

The strangest fact about the production is what it was built not to do. This was a summer release from a major studio, opening at number one with $30.1 million on 2,629 screens, and it was engineered to leave its story exactly where it found it. It sits between the fifth and sixth seasons; Mulder and Scully end it reassigned to the X-Files, the conspiracy intact, the colonization date unmoved. Chris Carter wrote the screenplay from a story he developed with Frank Spotnitz, and the production ran under the code name Blackwood with a crew largely drawn from the series. According to American Cinematographer's July 1998 production story, established feature directors expressed interest, and Carter wanted Rob Bowman anyway: a producer-director who had been with the show since its first season and had 23 episodes behind him, and in whom Carter had planted the idea of a film back in season three.

That continuity was a commercial bet, and it paid: $83.9 million domestic, $189.2 million worldwide, on a budget reported at $60 million by the wire services on opening weekend and at $66 million by The Numbers today. But it also meant the film could not do the one thing features do that television cannot, which is end. A movie that must hand its characters back unchanged can only enlarge the middle. So the money went to the middle.

The grammar that traveled

Bowman and his cinematographer, Ward Russell, told American Cinematographer they settled on three principles: motivated lighting, an active camera, and restrained color. The first and third are the series' own rules. Russell was a late hire; Bowman has said prep started so late that the top feature cinematographers were already committed to other summer pictures. Russell had come up as Tony Scott's gaffer before photographing Days of Thunder and The Last Boy Scout for him, and he kept the show's darkness, lighting even bright scenes by surrounding the foreground with black. The film was shot in Super 35 rather than anamorphic, on fast 500-speed stock chosen for a schedule heavy with night work. The widescreen frame came from cropping a spherical image, not from the squeezed anamorphic glass of so many 1998 blockbusters, and the result is a 2.35:1 picture that still composes like a television one.

Watch what that does in the Dallas sequence. Mulder and Scully are pulled off a bomb threat and sent to search the wrong building. The scene is a corridor, a flashlight, a vending machine, and a man who decides to stay with the bomb. It is composed exactly the way the series composed its best hours: a hard single source, black at the edges, two agents arguing about what they are looking at. It is the most frightening passage in the film, and it is also the cheapest.

The same is true of the hallway outside Mulder's apartment. Scully announces she is quitting. Mulder tells her she saved him, that he does not want to do this without her. The camera barely moves. The two actors stand a foot apart in a frame that could have been shot on any Tuesday in Vancouver. The scene ends with a bee sting instead of a kiss, which is the show's structural joke in miniature: the thing you want is always interrupted by the thing the plot needs. Nothing in the film's last forty minutes lands as hard as that interruption.

Where the money went

Then the film leaves the building. The Texas cornfields under their white domes are a genuine image, but the helicopter chase that follows is a sequence any action unit could have shot, and it looks like it. Storyboard artist Gabriel Hardman, interviewed on the Hey Danny It's Mulder podcast, has described the planning: the hive interiors needed wide lenses to show scale, the bees ended up computer-generated, and a separate car chase modeled on The French Connection, in which Mulder was to pursue the ambulance carrying Scully away and which the script itself called the car chase of Rob Bowman's dreams, was cut because the movie already had too much. The picture was overfull of exactly the material it was least suited to.

Antarctica is the proof. The craft under the ice is a large, well-built set, and the sequence is competently staged, but the series' first principle, that you never quite see the thing, is abandoned. We see the thing. We see the ship rise through the ice in a wide shot held for seconds. The moment that was supposed to be the payoff for five seasons of withholding is the moment the film becomes indistinguishable from every other effects picture of 1998. The frame that had been composed around absence is suddenly full, and fullness is the one thing this franchise never knew how to make frightening.

Russell said that very little in the film was static; even on a dolly the camera was usually moving. That is a Tony Scott habit, and it serves the Dallas search and the alley scenes with Martin Landau's jittery informant well. In the third act it becomes a tell. The camera keeps moving because there is nothing in the composition for it to withhold.

The actors it imported

The casting shows the same split. Landau's Kurtzweil belongs to the show's tradition of nervous informants, and his scenes with Duchovny, in a bar and a dark alley, are television scenes: two men, one secret, a frame that keeps the street behind them out of focus. Blythe Danner's Jana Cassidy, running the hearings that bracket the film, is a bureaucrat shot the way the series shot Skinner's superiors, with institutional light and no warmth. Armin Mueller-Stahl's Strughold, by contrast, is a movie villain, introduced at the Syndicate's London meeting and dispatched to a Tunisian cornfield for the coda. He is the one piece of casting that feels imported from another picture, and his scenes are the ones that could be cut without loss.

What it taught

The lesson was learned, eventually. The second film, a decade later, went small, and it is reconsidered elsewhere on this site. But the lesson was available in 1998 to anyone who watched Fight the Future with the sound off. The sequences that read as X-Files are dark, close and nearly motionless. The ones that read as summer product are bright and wide and never stop moving. The film is a controlled experiment in which the show's grammar and the blockbuster's grammar are run side by side for two hours, and the show wins every round it is allowed to fight.

Stark was right, then, just not in the way he meant. It is an episode with a big budget, and the budget is what is wrong with it.
