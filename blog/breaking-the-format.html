Who Holds the Camera

Seven episodes broke the show's format. Two of them, Chris Carter's, are an author showing his hand. The other five give the camera to a narrator nobody should trust: a novelist's informants, two bickering partners, a woman stuck in a Monday, a reality crew, the machines in a sushi bar.

October 16, 20266 min read1,417 words

Jose Chung, a novelist played by Charles Nelson Reilly, spends an hour of The X-Files interviewing people about an abduction in a small town, and every scene the episode shows is somebody's claim. A teenager under hypnosis describes aliens; a second session replaces them with Air Force men who erase her memory. A man named Roky insists he saw a creature from the earth's core he calls Lord Kinbote. A UFO obsessive describes Mulder and Scully as men in black. Scully gives Chung her own version, and even she cannot say what Mulder did at a diner.

Jose Chung's From Outer Space belongs on the list of episodes that broke the show's format, with Bad Blood, Triangle, The Post-Modern Prometheus, Monday, X-Cops and, from the revival, Rm9sbG93ZXJz. The seven get discussed as one tradition, the show letting its hair down, and the grouping covers a split. Two of them, Triangle and The Post-Modern Prometheus, were written and directed by Chris Carter and are director's episodes in the fullest sense: a Steadicam held for minutes at a stretch, a palette drained to black and white. The camera knows everything and shows you what the author decides. The other five give the camera, or the story the camera is illustrating, to a narrator who is partial, confused, exhausted, working to someone else's rules, or not a person. The X-Files was a show about witnesses who could not be trusted, and these five hours turned the episode itself into a witness. Television comedy still runs on what they worked out.

Jose Chung, April 1996

Darin Morgan wrote the founding document and Rob Bowman directed it; it first aired on April 12, 1996. The format experiment is in where the camera sits. The show's standard episode had a single trustworthy eye: the camera showed a monster, and the agents' job was to catch up. Morgan removed the trustworthy eye. He kept the furniture, the hypnotist, the eyewitness, the government men, and made all of it equally dubious, so that the hour ends with Chung at his typewriter concluding that on this planet "we are all alone." Emily St. James, in The A.V. Club, called it one of the very finest episodes of television she had seen while doubting it was a terrific episode of The X-Files. The doubt is earned: the hour is about what the show's camera had been pretending to know.

Bad Blood

Vince Gilligan's Bad Blood, directed by Cliff Bole and aired February 22, 1998, makes Morgan's move intimate. Mulder has staked a Texas pizza boy he believed was a vampire. On the way up to Skinner's office the partners rehearse their stories, and we see the case twice, first as Scully remembers it and then as Mulder does. Scully's version has a fast-talking, insufferable Mulder and a handsome sheriff, played by Luke Wilson, who takes an interest in her science. Mulder's version gives the same sheriff buck teeth and has Mulder patiently explaining vampire lore to a partner who will not listen. Whether Scully's shots hit the pizza boy changes depending on who is telling it.

The craft is in the control of mismatch. Each retelling has to be the same scene, same set, same blocking, with the acting recalibrated. Gillian Anderson plays Scully as the put-upon adult in her own telling and as a sulking skeptic in Mulder's, and the comedy lives in the gap. Zack Handlen, reviewing it for The A.V. Club, noted that unlike Rashomon the two tellings barely differ on events; the differences are in the details. The camera has stopped recording the world and started recording self-regard, and the show delivered that as farce.

Monday

Monday, written by Gilligan and John Shiban and directed by Kim Manners, aired February 28, 1999. Mulder's waterbed leaks, he needs to deposit a paycheck, and he walks into a bank where a robber named Bernard is wearing a bomb. The day repeats. Mulder does not know it. Only Bernard's girlfriend, Pam, knows, and she has lived this Monday more times than she can count and has stopped believing it can change.

The camera stays loyal to the agents' experience, which is why each loop looks like a first time, but the episode's truth belongs to Pam, who sits outside the bank watching variations the agents cannot perceive. Handlen set her against Bill Murray in Groundhog Day, who had a whole day to play with and passed through indulgence toward growth; Pam gets six hours at most, by his estimate, and is stuck in despair. The loop ends when she steps in front of a bullet meant for Mulder, which leaves the episode as Bad Blood with the second narrator removed: we are shown one version over and over and told, by the one person who knows, that it is a lie.

X-Cops

Gilligan has said he wore Carter down on X-Cops over several years, until Carter told him, "I'm tired of hearing about it. Just go ahead and do it." The episode aired on February 20, 2000, directed by Michael Watkins, and it handed the show's camera to another show entirely. The agents are followed through a night in Los Angeles by a documentary crew, in the house style of COPS. Gilligan told the Television Academy that he wanted the hour to look as much like COPS as possible, so it was shot on video instead of the usual 35mm stock, and that Bertram van Munster, a COPS producer and cameraman, photographed a fair bit of it himself. Watkins told Entertainment Weekly before it aired that the commitment was "to be Cops no matter what," and the magazine reported that the single video camera and the continuous takes saved, by Carter's estimate, about half a million dollars against the cost of a normal episode.

The reality format has rules the show had never obeyed: no music, no cutaways, available light, a camera the subjects can see. Scully spends the hour visibly resenting the crew. The creature, which seems to take the shape of each victim's fear, is barely glimpsed, because the format cannot show what the operator did not catch, and a sketch artist's rendering of it comes out looking like Freddy Krueger. The X-Files had always withheld its monsters by choice. X-Cops withholds them by rule, and the rule belongs to someone else. Handlen wrote that the video footage, flat and ugly as it is, gets past our defenses.

Rm9sbG93ZXJz

The revival's experiment came from the same lineage, though not from Gilligan. Rm9sbG93ZXJz, written by Kristen Cloke and Shannon Hamblin and directed by Glen Morgan, aired on February 28, 2018. The fan archive EatTheCorn, summarizing podcast interviews with the writers, has Cloke recalling that Morgan wanted four things in it: the Tay tweets, drones, artificial intelligence and no dialogue. The result opens with a computer voice narrating the fate of a chatbot that learned too much from its followers, then puts Mulder and Scully in an automated sushi bar, empty except for them, where Mulder refuses to tip and the machines decline to accept the decision. The title is base64 for "Followers."

Morgan shoots much of it from the machines' side, through security cameras and drones, in clean, polished spaces. The camera has become the antagonist, and it watches the agents the way the show once watched its monsters. Handlen described the episode as playing out a bit like a silent film comedy, which is right: without speech, the hour depends on two actors with twenty-five years of physical shorthand and on an audience trained to read their faces in the dark.

Carter's two

The long take in Triangle and the monochrome of The Post-Modern Prometheus are an author showing you his hand, and both hours are the better for the confidence. The writers' episodes hide the hand and ask whose it is. A novelist's informants, two rival partners, a woman trapped in a day, a reality crew, a network of appliances: each hour names the eye we are seeing through and makes that eye the problem. The comic procedural of the last two decades, with its mockumentary crews and its episodes narrated by the least reliable person in the room, runs on what The X-Files worked out here by turning its subject on itself.

Cloke, in the same podcast summary, says the finished Rm9sbG93ZXJz still had too much dialogue, that David Duchovny was funny and his improvised lines stayed in, and that they could have been more disciplined.
