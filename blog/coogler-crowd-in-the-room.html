A Crowd in the Room

The X-Files made its dread from two people, a flashlight and a cut. Ryan Coogler makes his from an unbroken camera moving through a full room. If the Hulu pilot carries his habits, that is a bigger break with the original than any new name on a badge.

October 22, 20266 min read1,488 words

Most hours of The X-Files could be cast with five people. Two agents, a witness, a local cop, something in the dark. Extras turned up when a script needed a town meeting or a hospital corridor, but the show was built for scarcity: a hallway, one beam, and a cut that lands before the beam reaches the far wall. The fear depended on how few people were in the room and how little of the room they were allowed to see.

Ryan Coogler's signature runs the other way. His best-known shots gather people into an enclosed space and then decline to cut. On Tuesday, October 13, Hulu picked his X-Files pilot up to series, with Danielle Deadwyler and Himesh Patel starring, Jennifer Yale as showrunner and Chris Carter among the executive producers. Coogler wrote, directed and executive produced the pilot. Autumn Durald Arkapaw photographed it and Ludwig Göransson scored it, the same pair who took home Oscars for Sinners on March 15. The commentary since has fixed on who the two new agents are. The larger question is how many bodies will share a frame with them, and whether anything cuts between those bodies. A show that keeps its camera moving through a populated room has changed the original's method more deeply than a show that renames its leads.

Two people and a beam

Ice, the first-season hour set at an Arctic research station, states the old rule as well as any hour the show made. James Wong told Starlog in 1995 that the writers needed a contained episode because "our shows were going over budget," and Glen Morgan said the set turned out "much bigger than we thought," though Starlog's writer found it still read as cramped on film. Carter has said, in a video interview that Collider links, that the film versions of John W. Campbell's novella inspired the hour, and the debt to John Carpenter's The Thing is obvious. A handful of scientists, two agents, a parasite that moves under the skin, and nobody steps outside. The dread is paranoia spread across six faces, handled in coverage: shot, reverse shot, a stare held a beat too long, a cut that lands like an accusation.

Duane Barry, a year later, had the ingredients of a crowd scene and refused them. An escaped psychiatric patient takes his psychiatrist and then the staff of a travel agency hostage; a tactical team sets up outside; Mulder is sent in to negotiate. It was Carter's first time directing, and what he built from a room full of hostages was, in Zack Handlen's phrase at The A.V. Club, an hour of "claustrophobic negotiations and visions." The episode lives on the line between two men, Mulder and Steve Railsback's Barry, with the hostages as furniture and the agents outside as an audience. When Mulder trades himself for a wounded hostage, the room gets emptier still. Even with a siege on the page, the show's instinct was to reduce the space to a pair.

Three long takes

Coogler's long takes have a history, and the history is a struggle with this problem: how many people a continuous shot can hold. The fight in Creed was shot on the third day of production, thirteen times, with take eleven in the film. Maryse Alberti was the cinematographer, Steadicam operator Benjamin Semanoff took boxing lessons to prepare, Michael B. Jordan spent about a week blocking the round with the boxer Gabriel Rosado, and Coogler shot no cutaways. The crowd is there, roaring at the rim of the frame, but the shot's subject is two bodies in a ring. Formally it is closer to Ice than to anything Coogler made afterward: isolation, held.

The crowd beat him once. On Black Panther, Rachel Morrison told the Motion Picture Association that the casino brawl was planned as one take and abandoned because "you had to understand where people were." It ended with six or seven cuts. She also described the instinct underneath: "Ryan's sensibility is to linger as much as you can," so that the audience feels part of something instead of watching it. Morrison's sentence names the opposite instinct from the old show's, which kept its audience outside the room, watching.

Sinners is where the crowd fit inside the shot. Mark Dillon's report in American Cinematographer describes the opening grocery-store sequence as a dialogue-heavy oner on a Steadicam operated by Renard Cheren, following Smoke into the store, sending a girl across the street to fetch her mother and walking the two of them back. Arkapaw said it "gives the viewer a good sense of the town" and that getting it was very important to Coogler. Then the juke joint: three minutes and twenty seconds, five shots stitched together, an Imax camera on a Steadicam circling the dancers while Sammie sings, as musicians from other eras appear among the patrons, the camera tilts to a burning roof and an exterior dolly pulls back from the dancers. To do it, the floor was cleared of everyone except performers, the operator and a spotter; the focus puller worked from a catwalk; the camera was reloaded after each 75-second take. Arkapaw and her gaffer lit the room with toplight and practicals so the camera could turn in any direction. "It was important for everything to feel real, not lit," she said.

John Bartley, in 1993, lit walls and let the actors find a flashlight. Arkapaw lit a ceiling so that no direction was off limits. Bartley's method located fear in whatever the beam had not reached yet; Arkapaw's puts everyone in the room on screen at once.

The reference films point the same way. Coogler and Arkapaw discussed The Thing, in AC's words, "for characters terrorized by an alien force in a contained environment." Carter named the same lineage for Ice. Two productions reached for one picture and came away with different parts of it. Ice took the paranoia and gave it to six people with a cut between every glance. Sinners took the siege and gave it to a room: the juke, the production's largest set, is where the partygoers barricade themselves against the vampires outside. Coogler's own explanation of why the film kept growing in format uses the word the old show would have claimed for itself. The flat country around Clarksdale, he told AC, is horizon and crops as far as the eye can see, and what a wider frame could carry was the feeling that "nobody's coming to help," the "paranoia that isolation can bring." He is describing a landscape, and it is the same fear the juke sequence stages indoors, with a crowd in the shot.

An X-File in one take

An X-File staged this way would walk the two agents into a church or a hospital ward and stay with them while the room went on being a room. The town would get a face before the monster did. The investigator would stop being the cone of light and become one body among thirty, no better lit than the rest. The original's cut was how the show looked away so that you would not see. A take that will not end has nowhere to look away to, so the thing in the room is in the shot with everyone else, and you do not know which of them it is.

The limits

None of this has been seen. The claim that the pilot was shot on 65mm began with a blog post in July and has been confirmed by neither Hulu nor any trade, as Series Order: Hulu Signs for a Season set out; what Arkapaw shot it on is unknown. If the rumor is true, it comes with constraints AC spelled out for Sinners: Imax takes run about two and a half minutes per roll and the cameras are too loud for sync sound, so a long dialogue take would go to the quieter System 65 cameras, as the grocery oner did. Jennifer Yale runs the series. Coogler directed the pilot; whether he directs again, and who else does, has not been announced. Arkapaw is credited on the pilot alone. The juke sequence itself is five shots, not one. Coogler cuts, and he assumed the editors could stitch Creed together from the takes if the single shot failed.

The original also had hours that filled the frame. Duane Barry had a siege, and the Hoover Building in Triangle was packed. What is at issue is the default, what the camera does on an ordinary Tuesday when nothing special has been scheduled. For nine seasons the default was the empty room. Hulu has bought a pilot from a director whose habit is the crowd, for a show whose habit was the pair, and the pilot was shot between May 11 and June 11 in Vancouver, the city where Bartley lit the first season's hallways. The first episode will show which habit gave.
