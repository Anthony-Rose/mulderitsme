Kim Manners Was Not a Horror Director

He directed more hours of The X-Files than anyone, and he is remembered for Home. The show itself kept hiring him for something else: speed.

October 16, 20266 min read1,319 words

Kim Manners knew what the fans called him. He told Fangoria in 2000 that they had him pegged as "the king of gore and the king of scary," and he did not argue. Home is his, and Home is the episode people still warn each other about. The label has hardened into the standard account: the show's writers supplied the ideas, and Manners supplied the dread.

I think that account is wrong, or at least that it gets the proportions backward. Watch his 52 episodes as a body of work and the fear is a side effect. What the show kept asking Manners for, from his first hour in 1995 to the series finale in 2002, was velocity. He was the director the producers reached for when an episode had to move, and his real contribution was teaching a series built on stillness how to run without losing its nerve.

Hired for speed

The hiring story matters, and Manners told it more than once. He came up through Stephen J. Cannell's shop, directing 21 Jump Street and Wiseguy in Vancouver, after years of action television that included Charlie's Angels. He had tried to get onto the show in its first season and found it, in his words, impossible to crack. In the version he gave The X-Files Magazine in 2002, he met Chris Carter in the bar of the Vancouver hotel where the crew stayed; Carter and Bob Goodwin screened a 21 Jump Street episode he had shot from a Glen Morgan and James Wong script, and he was given their Die Hand Die Verletzt in season two. Goodwin, then a co-executive producer, warned him that footage on this show tended to get reshot. Manners decided no one would reshoot his, and about two weeks after he wrapped he was invited back as a producer.

Nothing in that story is about fear. It is the story of a producer finding someone who could deliver a clean, finished hour on a Vancouver schedule. Screenwriter John August, who hired him years later to shoot the pilot of his low-budget web series Alaska, remembered him calling out "Two cameras, no waiting!" when he found an angle that let him shoot two close-ups at once. In 2002, shooting the finale under last-minute rewrites, his line to The X-Files Magazine was "We're winging it, buddy!" The man described in those accounts is a problem-solver with a cowboy's temperament, not a stylist brooding over shadows.

What Home actually does

Home is the exhibit everyone uses, so take it seriously. The famous sequence is the murder of the sheriff and his wife, scored to a Johnny Mathis sound-alike singing "Wonderful! Wonderful!" after Mathis read the script and refused to license his recording. Manners wanted that song because, as he put it, certain songs have a creepy quality no one openly acknowledges. The point is that the horror of the scene is a matter of rhythm and juxtaposition, not of what the camera shows. The Peacocks' white Cadillac rolls up at a leisurely pace. The beating is glimpsed, not studied. The music does the damage.

Manners himself talked about the episode as an attempt at a horror classic in the manner of the films he grew up on; he named Lon Chaney Jr. as his childhood hero and compared the hour to House on Haunted Hill. That is how a craftsman talks about a tradition he was serving. The terror was the writers' stated goal: Wong told The New Yorker that he and Morgan "were trying to make a terrifying show." The episode drew 18.85 million viewers and carried the series' first viewer discretion warning. According to Fangoria, Fox then kept it off the network until a single Halloween 1999 broadcast that was promoted as too controversial to have aired for three years. Its notoriety belongs to the writers' material. Its pace belongs to Manners.

The comedy test

The better evidence for what Manners was is Humbug, his second episode. Darin Morgan's script about retired sideshow performers was the show's first outright comedy, and Manners, a brand-new producer, admitted the whole crew was swimming in uncharted waters. What he did was refuse to wink. The episode is staged with the same sober framing and deliberate tempo as his horror hours, and the comedy comes through on its own. That is the same instinct that governs Home: trust the material, keep the camera honest, and control the tempo.

Morgan returned the favor in Jose Chung's From Outer Space, naming a profanity-spewing police detective "Detective Manners" after the director's speech patterns. Manners had planned to play the part himself, backed out, and later said he could have done it better than the actor who did. The joke only works if the man was known on set for his mouth and his momentum, not his menace.

The finales

Here is the part of the record the horror label cannot explain. From the seventh season onward, Manners directed the show's most structurally important hours: the season seven finale Requiem, the season eight premiere Within and finale Existence, and the two-hour series finale The Truth. These are mythology episodes, and the mythology was never scary. It was dense, talky, and in constant danger of stalling. It needed a director who could make exposition feel like pursuit.

The Truth is the clearest case. Manners described it to The X-Files Magazine as a courtroom drama for its first hour and ten minutes that then becomes a chase. That is a brutal structure to film: an hour of people sitting and testifying, then a sprint. He called directing it "the epitome of the eight years I've been on the show," and the phrase is revealing. The job he was proudest of had no monster in it at all.

Even his smaller favorites point the same way. He singled out Milagro, a character study about a writer whose creation seduces Scully, and Monday, the bank-robbery hour that tells its story four times. Neither depends on fright. Both depend on a director who can vary the pace of nearly identical material so that repetition reads as pressure rather than padding.

The Academy's silence

The Television Academy never nominated Manners for directing. Across the show's run its only directing nominations went to James Wong in 1997 and to Carter in 1998. Manners appears on the Academy's records only as a producer on four Outstanding Drama Series nominations, 1995 through 1998.

That is not an injustice so much as a category error. The Academy rewards the episode that looks like an event. Manners's skill was making 52 hours look like one show, which is the kind of work that becomes invisible the moment it succeeds. He said as much himself. "Robby and I set a real different look for the show," he told DGA Magazine in 2002, meaning Rob Bowman, the other producer-director who alternated with him for years; and in the same season he credited the lighting itself to John Bartley, Joel Ransom and Bill Roe. He was careful not to claim the darkness. He claimed the consistency.

Afterward

He went to Supernatural in 2005 as an executive producer and director, running the Vancouver production and, by Warner Bros.' account, directing more of its episodes than anyone else, including every season finale. His last two were the fourth-season premiere and an episode that fall. He died of cancer in Los Angeles on January 25, 2009, at 58. Eric Kripke called him "a brilliant director; more than that, he was a mentor and friend." When The X-Files returned in 2016, Morgan's Mulder and Scully Meet the Were-Monster sent Mulder into a cemetery to lay flowers at a headstone bearing Manners's name and the phrase he yelled at crews for years in place of "action": "Let's kick it in the ass."

It is the right epitaph, and no horror director would have chosen it.
