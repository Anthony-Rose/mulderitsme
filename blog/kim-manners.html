Kim Manners Was Hired for Speed

Kim Manners directed 52 hours of The X-Files, more than anyone else, and the producers kept handing him the hours that had to move: the first comedy, the finales, the two-hour close. Home, the episode he is remembered for, was the exception.

October 16, 20265 min read1,216 words

In 1993 Kim Manners was in Vancouver shooting The Adventures of Brisco County Jr. when he met Chris Carter in a hotel bar and told him, "I really want to do your show." It took a while. "I tried very hard to get on The X-Files during the first season," he told Fangoria in 2000, "and for a lot of reasons I found it impossible to crack." Carter screened a 21 Jump Street episode Manners had directed and gave him Die Hand Die Verletzt in season two. Bob Goodwin, then a co-executive producer, warned him that footage on this show tended to get reshot. Manners decided no one would reshoot his, and about two weeks after he wrapped he was invited back as a producer.

The hiring is the story of a producer finding someone who could deliver a clean, finished hour on a Vancouver schedule. The fans filed him differently. "The fans have got me pegged as the king of gore and the king of scary," he said in the same Fangoria interview, and Home is the reason: his, and still the episode people warn each other about. Watch his 52 hours as a body of work, though, and the fear is a side effect. What the show kept asking Manners for, from his first hour in 1995 to the series finale in 2002, was velocity.

Two cameras, no waiting

Manners came up through Stephen J. Cannell's shop, directing 21 Jump Street and Wiseguy in Vancouver after years of action television that began with Charlie's Angels. Screenwriter John August, who met him when Manners directed the pilot of Alaska, a frontier series August was writing on that never got picked up, remembered him hollering "Two cameras, no waiting!" whenever he found an angle that let him shoot two close-ups at once. In 2002, shooting the finale under last-minute rewrites, his line to The X-Files Magazine was "We're winging it, buddy!" The man in those accounts solves problems at a cowboy's pace.

Home

Home is the exhibit everyone uses, so it deserves a close look. The famous sequence is the murder of the sheriff and his wife, scored to a Johnny Mathis sound-alike singing "Wonderful! Wonderful!" after Mathis read the script and refused to license his recording. Manners wanted that song because, as he put it in an interview quoted in Matt Hurwitz and Chris Knowles's The Complete X-Files, certain songs have "a creepy, icky quality that none of us have really openly acknowledged." The horror of the scene lives in rhythm and juxtaposition. The Peacocks' white Cadillac rolls up at a leisurely pace. The beating is glimpsed. The music does the damage.

Manners talked about the episode as an attempt at a horror classic in the manner of the films he grew up on. "Lon Chaney Jr. was my hero growing up," he said, and he thought back to House on Haunted Hill while shooting. The terror was the writers' stated goal: James Wong told The New Yorker that he and Glen Morgan "were trying to make a terrifying show." The episode drew 18.85 million viewers and carried the series' first viewer discretion warning. Fox then kept it off the network until a single Halloween 1999 broadcast, promoted, by Fangoria's account, as an episode "so controversial, it's been banned from television for three years." The writers earned the notoriety. What Manners added was the pace, and the pace is why the scene still works.

Humbug and Jose Chung

The better evidence for what Manners was is Humbug, his second episode. Darin Morgan's script about retired sideshow performers was the show's first outright comedy, and Manners, a brand-new producer, admitted later that he had been "very, very nervous to be handed the first comedy ever." "We were all swimming in uncharted waters," he said. He did not wink. The episode is staged with the same sober framing and deliberate tempo as his horror hours, and the comedy comes through on its own, which is the instinct that governs Home too: trust the material and control the tempo.

Morgan returned the favor in Jose Chung's From Outer Space with a profanity-spewing police detective named Detective Manners, written in the hope that the director would play him. Manners agreed, then backed out late in a season that had left him, in his words, dog-tired, and after seeing the episode he decided, "I could have done such a better job than that guy." The joke depends on a man known on set for his mouth and his momentum.

Requiem, Within, Existence, The Truth

From the seventh season onward Manners directed the hours the series could least afford to get wrong: the season seven finale Requiem, the season eight premiere Within and finale Existence, and the two-hour series finale The Truth. These are mythology episodes, and the mythology was dense, talky and in constant danger of stalling. It needed a director who could make exposition feel like pursuit.

The Truth is the hardest case. The X-Files Magazine described it as a courtroom drama for its first hour and ten minutes that then becomes a chase: an hour of people sitting and testifying, then a sprint. Manners called directing it "the epitome of the eight years I've been on the show." The job he was proudest of had no monster in it.

Even his smaller favorites point the same way. He singled out Milagro, about a writer whose creation seduces Scully, and Monday, which he described as "a bank robbery story told four different times." Neither depends on fright. Both depend on a director who can vary the pace of nearly identical material so that repetition reads as pressure.

The Academy

The Television Academy never nominated Manners for directing. Across the show's run its directing nominations went to James Wong in 1997 and to Carter in 1998. Manners appears in the Academy's records as a producer on four Outstanding Drama Series nominations, 1995 through 1998.

The Academy rewards the episode that looks like an event. Manners's skill was making 52 hours look like one show, and that kind of work disappears the moment it succeeds. He said as much himself. "Robby and I set a real different look for the show," he told DGA Magazine in 2002, meaning Rob Bowman, the other producer-director who alternated with him for years. To The X-Files Magazine that September he added, "We had John Bartley, Joel Ransom and Bill Roe, who lit the show beautifully." He claimed the consistency and left the darkness to the cinematographers.

Supernatural

Manners went to Supernatural in 2005 as an executive producer and director. MovieWeb reported at his death that he had directed more of its episodes than any other director, every season finale included. His last two were the fourth-season premiere, Lazarus Rising, and Metamorphosis that fall. He died of cancer in Los Angeles on January 25, 2009, at 58. Eric Kripke called him "a brilliant director; more than that, he was a mentor and friend." When The X-Files returned in 2016, Darin Morgan's Mulder and Scully Meet the Were-Monster sat Mulder in a cemetery against a headstone bearing Manners's name, his real dates, and the phrase he yelled at crews for years in place of "action": "Let's kick it in the ass."
