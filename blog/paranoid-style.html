The Paranoid Style, Renewed Weekly

The X-Files took its parking garages and sodium light from the conspiracy thrillers of the 1970s. Those films ended in defeat inside two hours. A weekly series needed paranoia that could come back on Friday, and the model for that was a cancelled ABC show about a Chicago reporter.

October 16, 20266 min read1,286 words

Alan J. Pakula's The Parallax View (1974) ends with its reporter shot in a convention hall and a committee ruling that he acted alone. Sydney Pollack's Three Days of the Condor (1975) ends on a sidewalk, with Robert Redford saying he has given his story to The New York Times and Cliff Robertson asking how he knows they will print it. Even All the President's Men (1976), the one with a victory in it, keeps its two reporters small inside a bright newsroom while the truth lives in a garage. The paranoid thriller of that decade is a terminal form. The hero learns the shape of the system, the system closes over him, and the picture is finished in two hours.

Chris Carter grew up on Watergate and these films, borrowed their informants and their underlit garages, and moved the apparatus into a basement at the FBI. The show never hid the debt; Mulder's first source is named Deep Throat. What the show could not borrow was the ending. A weekly series has to be paranoid again on Friday, so The X-Files kept the images of the 1970s thriller and threw away their conclusion. Those films were about losing. The series was about never being allowed to finish, and that difference in plotting explains more about it than any list of influences.

Gordon Willis's frame

Willis photographed both The Parallax View and All the President's Men, and the nickname the writer Jon Fauer credits to Conrad Hall, the Prince of Darkness, has followed him since. The nickname misses that Willis's darkness was an argument about scale. The American Society of Cinematographers, looking back at The Parallax View, credited him with "an unerring sense of anamorphic composition," and the composition is where the film's politics live. Warren Beatty's Joe Frady is repeatedly placed low and off center in enormous architecture, caught on the catwalks of a convention center, dwarfed by the Space Needle where the film's first assassination happens. The same ASC retrospective describes the finish: an assassin steps through a door into a blinding blast of light, the shots are heard and not seen, and a committee in the coda declares that Frady acted alone. A man that small in a frame that large does not win, and the composition tells you so before the plot does.

The X-Files took the darkness and left the scale. Its people are close, often in two-shot, lit by what they carry. The hallways are long but narrow, and the conspiracy, when it appears, is a handful of older men in a room with a table. Pakula and Willis composed power as architecture. The series composed it as a cigarette, a more intimate image of corruption and a more renewable one. A man can come back next season; a building, once brought down, cannot.

Deep Throat, twice

The informant is the borrowing everyone notices. In All the President's Men Hal Holbrook's Deep Throat meets Woodward in a garage that Pakula and Willis treat as the place where truth is dragged slowly toward light, against a fluorescent newsroom that Willis found oppressive and lit anyway. Holbrook told The Washington Post in 2016 that, standing in that garage, he was "literally and figuratively in the dark" about the man he was playing. The character is a voice and a silhouette. He stands outside the story's institutions, which is what lets him tell the truth about them.

Carter's Deep Throat, played by Jerry Hardin, is already inside. He is a conscience within the system talking to another employee of the same system. The other obvious ancestor is the informant Donald Sutherland plays in Oliver Stone's JFK (1991), and when Hardin's character is shot dead at the end of the first season, his replacement, Steven Williams's X, shares a name with Stone's informant, which is hard to read as coincidence. Something shifts in that transfer. Woodward is a journalist. Garrison, in Stone's film, is a district attorney. Mulder is a federal agent with a badge and a basement office, and his sources are colleagues. The 1970s thriller was about an outsider who finds a crack in the wall. The X-Files gave its insider a desk next to the crack and told him to keep quiet.

The move was necessary. Richard Hofstadter's essay in the November 1964 Harper's, which gave the style its name, described a mind for which "decisive events are not taken as part of the stream of history, but as the consequences of someone's will." Hofstadter was writing about politics, but the sentence describes a plotting method. If every event is willed, every event is a clue, and clues can be rationed indefinitely. A reporter who learns the whole truth has to publish or die. An agent who learns a piece of it every few weeks can be kept in a basement for nine years. The institution the show distrusts is also the device that keeps the story running.

Scully

The other place the show broke from its models was in who it put at the center. Pakula's and Pollack's films are stories about men, with women as victims or brief refuges. Carter told Smithsonian magazine in 2008 that The Silence of the Lambs was an inspiration and that it was "not a mistake" that Scully has red hair like Clarice Starling. The debt is usually read as a casting note, and it is also a change in the genre's point of view. Starling is inside the Bureau and under its discipline, and her intelligence is what the film thinks with. Making the skeptic a scientist and a woman inside the same institution as the believer gave The X-Files something the 1970s thrillers never had, an internal argument. Frady and Turner are alone with what they know. Mulder is never alone, and the person beside him does not believe him. The show's suspense lives in the car and the autopsy bay more than in the garage.

Kolchak

Carter's own account of the show's origin points somewhere else. He has said repeatedly that the series that inspired him was Kolchak: The Night Stalker, the ABC series that followed two hit television movies and ran twenty episodes between September 1974 and March 1975, with Darren McGavin as a Chicago reporter nobody believes. In a 1997 Rolling Stone interview, as /Film quotes it, he called it the show that inspired The X-Files; to Smithsonian in 2008 he described the two movies as "fantastic. Scary."

Kolchak is the structural parent, and it matters that it is a television parent. Its paranoia is renewable by design: a monster a week, an editor who does not believe him, a story spiked before the credits. McGavin had tired of it within the year. By his own later account, as the MeTV site relays it, he phoned the network himself and asked them to stop. The X-Files solved Kolchak's problem by giving it Pakula's furniture. The weekly monster stayed, but behind it ran a second story with the look and the vocabulary of the 1970s thriller, informants and garages and men in suits, so that the one-off creature episodes felt like fragments of something larger.

The pilot has the overhead sodium light, the figure half seen, the sense that the building knows more than the people in it. Its ending is where the films part company with it. Pakula's reporters lose, or win once. Carter's agents do neither. They go back to the basement, and the system that would have swallowed Joe Frady keeps Mulder on the payroll. Carter paid his debt to the other parent in kind: he cast McGavin in two episodes as Arthur Dales, a retired agent who had worked the first X-Files.
