The Paranoid Style, Renewed Weekly

The X-Files took its parking garages and its shadows from the conspiracy thrillers of the 1970s, then quietly reversed what those films meant. Pakula's paranoia ended in defeat. Television needed paranoia that could come back next week.

October 16, 20266 min read1,427 words

The received account of where The X-Files came from goes like this: Chris Carter grew up on Watergate and the thrillers it produced, borrowed their informants and their underlit garages, and moved the whole apparatus into a basement at the FBI. The account is true as far as it goes, and the show never hid it. Mulder's first source is literally named Deep Throat. But the lineage is usually told as inheritance, and it was closer to theft followed by inversion. The X-Files kept the images of the 1970s paranoid thriller and threw away their conclusion. Those films were about losing. The series was about never being allowed to finish.

That is a structural difference, not a tonal one, and it explains more about the show than any list of influences. Alan J. Pakula's The Parallax View (1974) ends with its reporter shot in a convention hall and a committee ruling that he acted alone. Sydney Pollack's Three Days of the Condor (1975) ends with Robert Redford claiming he has given his story to The New York Times and Cliff Robertson asking how he knows they will print it. Even All the President's Men (1976), the one with a victory in it, keeps its two reporters small inside a bright newsroom while the truth lives in a garage. The paranoid thriller of that decade is a terminal form. The hero learns the shape of the system, the system closes over him, and the picture is finished in two hours. A weekly series cannot do that. It has to be paranoid again on Friday.

What Pakula actually offered

Gordon Willis photographed both The Parallax View and All the President's Men, and the nickname usually credited to Conrad Hall, the Prince of Darkness, has followed him since. What the nickname misses is that Willis's darkness was an argument about scale. The American Society of Cinematographers, looking back at The Parallax View, credited him with "an unerring sense of anamorphic composition," and the composition is where the film's politics live. In it Warren Beatty's Joe Frady is repeatedly placed low and off center in enormous architecture, caught on the catwalks of a convention center, dwarfed by the Space Needle where the film's first assassination happens. The same ASC retrospective describes the finish bluntly: an assassin steps through a door into a blinding blast of light, the shots are heard rather than seen, and a committee in the coda declares that Frady acted alone. The composition tells you the outcome before the plot does. A man that small in a frame that large does not win.

The X-Files took the darkness and left the scale. Its people are not small in the frame. They are close, often in two-shot, lit by what they carry. The hallways in the series are long but they are also narrow, and the conspiracy, when it finally appears, is a handful of older men in a room with a table. Where Pakula and Willis composed power as architecture, the series composed it as a cigarette. That is a far more intimate image of corruption, and a far more renewable one, because a man can come back next season. A building cannot be defeated and then reappear.

The informant, moved indoors

The clearest borrowing is the informant, and the clearest reversal is what the show did with him. In All the President's Men Hal Holbrook's Deep Throat meets Woodward in a garage that Pakula and Willis treat as the place where truth is dragged slowly toward light, in deliberate contrast to the fluorescent newsroom that Willis found oppressive and used anyway. Holbrook later said he was "literally and figuratively in the dark" about the real man he was playing. The character is a voice and a silhouette. He is outside the story's institutions, which is the only reason he can tell the truth about them.

Carter's Deep Throat, played by Jerry Hardin, is already inside. He is not a leak from the system to a reporter; he is a conscience inside the system talking to another employee of the same system. The other obvious ancestor is the informant Donald Sutherland plays in Oliver Stone's JFK (1991), and when Hardin's character is shot dead at the end of the first season, his replacement, Steven Williams's X, shares a name with Stone's informant, which is hard to read as coincidence. Something has shifted in that transfer. Woodward is a journalist. Garrison, in Stone's film, is a district attorney. Mulder is a federal agent with a badge and a basement office, and his sources are colleagues. The 1970s paranoid thriller was about the outsider who finds a crack in the wall. The X-Files was about the insider who is given a desk next to the crack and told to keep quiet.

That move was necessary rather than clever. Richard Hofstadter's 1964 Harper's essay, which gave the style its name, described a mind for which "decisive events are not taken as part of the stream of history" but as the product of someone's will. Hofstadter was writing about politics, but the sentence describes a plotting method. If every event is willed, every event is a clue, and clues can be rationed indefinitely. A reporter who learns the whole truth has to publish or die. An agent who learns a piece of it every few weeks can be kept in a basement for nine years. The institution the show distrusts is also the device that keeps the story running.

The television ancestor

Carter's own account of the show's origin points somewhere else entirely. He has said repeatedly that the series that inspired him was Kolchak: The Night Stalker, the ABC series that followed two hit television movies and ran a single season in 1974 and 1975 with Darren McGavin as a Chicago reporter nobody believes. In a March 1997 Rolling Stone interview he called it the show that inspired The X-Files; to Smithsonian magazine in 2008 he described the two movies as "fantastic. Scary." He later cast McGavin in two episodes as a retired agent who had worked the first X-Files.

Kolchak is the structural parent, and it matters that it is a television parent. Its paranoia is renewable by design: a monster a week, an editor who does not believe him, a story that gets spiked before the credits. McGavin reportedly grew tired of the formula and the schedule, and the show stopped after twenty episodes. The X-Files solved Kolchak's problem by giving it Pakula's furniture. The weekly monster stayed, but behind it ran a second story with the look and the vocabulary of the 1970s thriller, informants and garages and men in suits, so that the one-off creature episodes felt like fragments of something larger. The genius of the hybrid was that the renewable part borrowed gravity from the terminal part without ever having to pay its bill.

Scully as the departure

The other place the show broke from its models was in who it put at the center. Pakula's and Pollack's films are stories about men, with women as victims or brief refuges. Carter has said that The Silence of the Lambs was an inspiration and that it was "not a mistake" that Scully has red hair like Clarice Starling. The debt is often read as a casting note. It is better read as a change in the genre's point of view. Starling is inside the Bureau and under its discipline, and her intelligence is the film's instrument. Making the skeptic a scientist and a woman inside the same institution as the believer gave The X-Files what the 1970s thrillers never had: an internal argument. Frady and Turner are alone with what they know. Mulder is never alone, and the person beside him does not believe him. That is where the show's suspense actually lives, in the car and the autopsy bay rather than the garage.

Watching the lineage

Put The Parallax View and All the President's Men beside the pilot and the borrowing is obvious: the overhead sodium light, the figure half seen, the sense that the building knows more than the people in it. Then watch the endings. Pakula's reporters either lose or win once. Carter's agents do neither. They go back to the basement, and the system that would have swallowed Joe Frady instead keeps Mulder on the payroll. The X-Files took the paranoid style and made it a condition rather than a verdict, which is why the look of those films survived in it long after their despair did not.
