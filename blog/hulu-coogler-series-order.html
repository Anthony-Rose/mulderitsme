Series Order: Hulu Signs for a Season

Eight months after ordering a pilot, Hulu has picked up Ryan Coogler's X-Files to series. What the announcement confirms, what the trades report, what the blogs claim, and what the shape of the deal says about the show.

October 16, 20266 min read1,446 words

On Tuesday, October 13, Hulu announced that it had picked up Ryan Coogler's The X-Files to series. The pilot had been ordered in February, which means the project passed through the oldest gate in American television, a single episode judged before anyone commits to more, at a time when streaming dramas usually go straight to series. The show had to earn its season. What follows reads the announcement for what it says and leaves the gaps empty.

Confirmed

The official description is modest. Two decorated FBI agents, temperamentally far apart, are assigned to a division that has been closed for years and that handles the cases nobody else can explain. Danielle Deadwyler and Himesh Patel star. Coogler wrote, directed and executive produced the pilot and is an executive producer on the series. Jennifer Yale is the showrunner. Zinzi Coogler and Sev Ohanian of Proximity Media are executive producers, as is Chris Carter. Simone Harris, Deadwyler and Patel are co-executive producers. The series comes from Onyx Collective and 20th Television in association with Proximity Media. There is no premiere date and no episode count; TheWrap guessed fall 2027 and labeled it a guess.

Coogler said, "What Chris Carter created is special," and that writing and directing the pilot "has meant a great deal to me." Proximity's founders said in the same announcement that Ludwig Göransson scored the pilot, and TheWrap reported that Autumn Durald Arkapaw, who won the Academy Award in March for photographing Sinners, shot it.

The confirmed list ends there. Everything else is reporting, rumor or interview, and the three piles belong apart.

Reported

The most consequential report is that Deadwyler and Patel are not playing Mulder and Scully. Variety attributed that to a source, and Deadline reported the same when Patel was cast in March. Nothing attributed to Hulu says it either way. The closest thing to an on-record confirmation is Patel himself, who told The Direct in early July that he and Deadwyler were playing "completely new characters." An actor describing his own role is worth more than an anonymous source and less than a studio statement.

The project began under Proximity's exclusive television deal with Disney, which Variety reported had expired by the time Proximity's television arm signed with Netflix in June. Carter first mentioned a Coogler version in March 2023, on a CBC radio program, so development has run more than three years. This series stays at Hulu.

Vancouver production listings put the shoot in British Columbia from May 11 to June 11, 2026, and Patel said in early July that the pilot had wrapped. The city is where the original shot its first five seasons. Deadline reported in May that Amy Madigan, Steve Buscemi, Ben Foster, Devery Jacobs and Tantoo Cardinal were among the guest cast. Patel and Deadwyler acted together in Station Eleven, he as Jeevan and she as Miranda; there is no reported prior collaboration between Patel and Coogler.

Rumored

The claims that the pilot runs about two hours and was shot on 65mm film began with a July 17 post on the blog World of Reel, which attributed the format to unnamed "reports." JoBlo, repeating the claim this week, cited an X account called Shot on Film. The same blog said in July that Coogler had asked Disney to consider releasing the pilot as a feature, and on the day of the series order it repeated the two-hour, 65mm description and added, on nothing firmer than "what I've heard," a claim about who appears in the episode. None of this has been confirmed by Hulu or by any trade. Arkapaw's involvement is confirmed; what she shot on is not.

The principals

Gillian Anderson, at Awesome Con in March, said she had had "a few conversations" with Coogler and that "the pilot script is really good." David Duchovny told The Hollywood Reporter in April that he had spoken to Coogler but had not read the script, that there was "nothing concrete at this point," and that he did not know whether his character exists in the show.

Carter's position is the odd one. In August, promoting his R-rated director's cut of I Want to Believe, subtitled Vrach Frankenshteyn and released on Hulu and Disney+ on August 14, he told The Hollywood Reporter that his producer credit reflected no work.

My name is on it as executive producer, but I have done exactly nothing.

He told Nerdist he had declined to read the script because "I want to be surprised," and told Polygon he thought Anderson was going to appear and assumed Duchovny would, while saying he did not know the details. A creator who has chosen not to read the script is not a source on its contents, and his guess about the cast is a guess.

Two agents, one division

Reduced to its parts, the description is the original's premise: two agents, one division, cases without explanations. The show ran on the argument between the two people in the car, one of whom needed the impossible to be true and one of whom needed it not to be, and every case was a referendum on which of them was right that week. If the reports of new characters hold, Coogler and Yale have kept the premise and replaced the drivers. The description's "different" carries more of the show than its "unexplained," and it does not say what divides these two. The old axis, faith against medicine, is one possibility among several.

New characters are also the most workable way to make the show. Carter's own revival seasons, which aired on Fox in 2016 and 2018, showed that Mulder and Scully's story had been finished more than once and that each reopening cost something. A series that has to carry decades of backstory into the room before it can open a case is a series about its own continuity. Two strangers in a reopened basement can open the case.

Format and frame

If the 65mm report is accurate, it is a counterintuitive choice for this property. The original was built on a flashlight, a dark room and a figure the lens could not resolve. Large-format film resolves everything and suits landscapes and faces at scale. Arkapaw used it on Sinners for a story that spread across a Mississippi day before collapsing into one building at night. An X-Files episode spends most of its life in the building. One possibility is that Coogler wants the clarity on purpose, that the new show's fear will come from how much you can see, and that the darkness of 1993 was a solution to a budget and not a law. That is speculation stacked on a rumor, and the sensible position is to wait for a frame.

Length is not speculation. A streaming season of eight or ten episodes, the likely shape even though Hulu has not said, changes the show's economy. The 22-episode network model had room for the outlier hours that made the series' reputation, the comedies and the format experiments, because the schedule had slack. A short season has none. Every hour has to advance something, and the standalone case becomes an expense instead of the default. The risk is a season that is all arc and no cases, the proportion the original got wrong in its last years. The opportunity is that a short season does not need a conspiracy to hold it together. Eight good cases and one working partnership would be enough, and closer to what made the show work than any long game.

The promissory note

The conspiracy is the trap the new show inherits. It ran as a promissory note, renewed every May and deferred every November, and by the end the show could not pay it. Any reboot that respects the original will be tempted to assume that debt, to plant the black oil and the Syndicate and a colonization date in episode two because that is what the brand sounds like. Carter's distance may turn out to be the best thing about this production. He is not in the room, he has not read the script, and nobody at the table is charged with defending the old continuity. Whether the show declines the note or signs for it again will be visible within a season.

Open questions

The cinematographer and composer of the pilot are confirmed; the director of episode two is not, and that hire will say whether the series intends to keep the pilot's look. The episode count, when announced, will say what kind of show Hulu thinks it bought. The first trailer will show whether anyone is carrying a flashlight.
