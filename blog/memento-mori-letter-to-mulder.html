A Letter She Meant to Throw Out

Memento Mori gives Scully the voiceover, and every word of it is addressed to Mulder. By morning he has read the journal she meant to destroy and pocketed a vial of her ova, and she has been told about one of the two.

November 26, 20266 min read1,298 words

The episode opens on an x-ray of a skull, and the camera moves in slowly toward Dana Scully as she studies it. Paula Vitaris, in Cinefantastique's October 1997 episode guide, called the image "a modern-day, technological memento mori," the tumor glowing in the sinus cavity. Over it Gillian Anderson reads long sentences about luminous mysteries and loosening tethers, and a clause in the middle of them gives away who they are for: "knowing that you will read them and share my burden." (Dialogue here comes from the unofficial transcript at Inside the X, typed out by a fan, and so do the descriptions of staging.)

Memento Mori aired on Fox on February 9, 1997, the fourteenth episode of the fourth season. Rob Bowman directed, Michael S. Stern edited, and the script carries four names: Chris Carter, Vince Gilligan, John Shiban and Frank Spotnitz.

Scully narrates three times in the hour, and each time she is writing to Mulder. The closest the episode comes to the inside of her head is a document with his name on it. Narration and plot both route her through Mulder, and both leave decisions about her body with men who do not tell her. By the last scene one man has read her journal and put a vial of her ova in his pocket, another has opened negotiations for her life, and she has been told about the journal.

The addressee

Within Scully's first few lines to Mulder, in an x-ray room at a Washington hospital, she puts the voiceover's "you" into six words: "You're the only one I've called." Her mother hears by way of him. Scully asks Mulder to phone her, and Margaret Scully arrives at the hospital in Allentown, Pennsylvania, angry that she was not told at once. The second stretch of narration runs under a radiation session and uses his name. The third finds her in the hospital bed, writing in the journal. By then the letter has said what it is for. It is to be read "if the darkness should have swallowed me," it tells him there was nothing he might have done, and it informs him that "this last distance must necessarily be traveled alone."

Emily St. James, grading the hour an A at The A.V. Club in 2011, called the narration "the purplest of purple prose" and the diary message "perhaps intentionally pretentious." Vitaris, who gave it two and a half stars, had Scully "writing platitudes in her journal." Scully tells her mother, "I don't have any experience being sick." She is a doctor drafting last words at the start of a diagnosis, to the first person she called, and she reaches for the diction of a eulogy. Out loud, in the same hour, she says "nasopharyngeal mass" and gives her odds as a statistic.

Three days before prep

Spotnitz told Maureen Ryan of The Huffington Post in 2013 that Darin Morgan, who had left the staff, was going to write one more episode, that Morgan phoned three or four days before prep to say he could not finish, and that the reaction in the office was, "We've got to have a script to prep." He, Shiban and Gilligan outlined and drafted, in three days by his telling, a cancer story the writers had been debating, and Carter wrote the script over Christmas. Spotnitz called the result "the best single mythology episode we ever did." Morgan's version differs. An interview he gave The X-Cast podcast, summarized by the fan archive EatTheCorn in January 2024, has him calling Spotnitz's story untrue: Carter had left him an open invitation and he never committed to it. Those are a fan site's notes on a recording. As for the letters, Spotnitz's telling leaves the last hand on the script with Carter, which points to him as their writer without settling it.

Dr. Scanlon's ward

Scully commits herself by telephone. Mulder is going through Betsy Hagopian's files, where he has just found a fertility clinic, when she calls from the hospital where Penny Northern is a patient and asks for her overnight bag, and she gives him the reason in his own vocabulary: "the truth is in me," and that is where she means to pursue it. The doctor she has chosen is Penny's, a man named Scanlon.

Bowman introduces him as a white glare that resolves into a face. He promises chemotherapy and radiation, "a high dose approach," and adds, "You're going to feel like dying." For the treatment she lies flat with a plastic mask over her face while the equipment turns around her head. Later she dreams of a spinning metal object coming at her face and wakes to Penny, the last survivor of the abductee group, holding her hand. "They let me come to you during the procedures," Penny says, meaning the other procedures, the ones Scully does not remember.

A patient lying flat, equipment circling her head, a metal object closing on her face, the same woman offering comfort: the ward is furnished like the abduction, and before long the plot catches up with the set. Mulder finds Scanlon's name on the staff of the Lombard Research Facility, where a clone explains that the abducted women were given high radiation to force superovulation. Scully has asked a doctor for the thing that was done to her.

The drawer at Lombard

From an argument in Kurt Crawford's apartment early in the hour until Byers opens a door on Penny's room near the end, Mulder and Scully share no scene on screen, and one phone call connects them. Bowman and Stern spend the middle cutting between her bed and his break-in. Scully writes that she needs to know he is out there. The next scene puts him in a vault, in front of a drawer with her name on it and vials of her ova inside, and he leaves with one.

Skinner gets a parallel errand. Mulder asks him to arrange a meeting with the Cigarette Smoking Man, saying he is ready to deal, and Skinner refuses twice with the same three words: "Find another way." Then Skinner meets the man himself and asks, "What would it take?" Vitaris thought that scene and the last hallway conversation were the two that played true, and she wrote of the series, "The male characters of this series have a lot to answer for."

5:05 a.m.

A title gives the time as 9:47 p.m. when Mulder reaches her room and finds it empty, the journal lying there, and he reads the latest entry. By the next title, 5:05 a.m., Penny is dead. Scully comes out into the corridor where Mulder has been waiting on a bench, and he admits what he read.

Her answer is a review: "I didn't want you to read that. I had decided to throw it out." The narrator disowns the narration, sounding a little like Vitaris, and then announces a plan in her speaking voice: "I came into this hospital able to work, and that's how I'm leaving." Further down the same entry, in a list of complaints about the cancer storyline, Vitaris called that resolution "sheer nonsense." Scully makes it without knowing what is in Mulder's pocket. He tells her the truth will save her and kisses her forehead, and only after she has walked off down the hall does the vial come out for him to look at.

At 5:30 he phones Skinner's office, expecting voice mail, and Skinner picks up. Mulder thanks him for the advice about his chain-smoking friend. "There's always another way," Skinner says, and hangs up. The Smoking Man, who is sitting across the desk, agrees that there is, "if you're willing to pay the price." He leaves his cigarette in a coffee cup.
