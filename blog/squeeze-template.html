CASE FILE 01 // MONSTER OF THE WEEK

Why "Squeeze" Still Works

Filed November 14, 20252 min readBy the Basement Desk
DECLASSIFIED

Three episodes into its first season, the show found the formula it would run on for nine years. It all starts with an air vent.

Plenty of shows take a season to figure out what they are. The X-Files figured it out in its third episode. "Squeeze" aired in September 1993, and it's the first time the show steps away from aliens and government cover-ups to tell a self-contained horror story. It became the blueprint for the "monster of the week" format that would carry the series through nine seasons.

The monster is a man

Eugene Victor Tooms doesn't look like a monster. He works for animal control, sits politely in an interview room, and passes a polygraph on the question that matters. That's what makes him frightening. The show figured out early that the scariest thing in a room is the person nobody suspects, and Doug Hutchison plays Tooms with an unnerving stillness that does more than any makeup could.

The supernatural details are simple and specific: he can stretch his body through impossible gaps, he needs five human livers, and he sleeps for thirty years between cycles. There's no long explanation. The show trusts you to find that more upsetting than a lecture would be.

Scully gets a choice

The other thing "Squeeze" does well is give Scully a reason to pick a side. An old Academy friend invites her onto a "real" case and quietly mocks her partner. She could take the easy career path. Instead she sticks with the basement. It's a small moment, but it establishes that her loyalty is to the truth, not to Mulder's theories, and that the two aren't always the same thing.

The template

Watch it now and you can see the parts every later monster episode borrowed:

  • A cold open that shows you the threat before the agents arrive.
  • A local authority who thinks the FBI is wasting everyone's time.
  • Mulder making a leap that sounds insane and turns out to be right.
  • Scully finding the physical evidence that makes the leap stick.
  • An ending that closes the case without fully closing the door.

That last one matters. Tooms is locked up at the end, but the final shot reminds you that walls don't mean much to him. He'd be back.

Where to go next

If "Squeeze" works for you, watch its sequel, "Tooms," later in season one. Then try "Ice" and "Darkness Falls." That's the early show at its best: small casts, isolated places, and a dread that comes from the budget limits as much as the script.
