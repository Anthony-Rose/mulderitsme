Two Solos: What the Leads Did With the Camera

Handed the director's chair, David Duchovny and Gillian Anderson each made the same decision. They removed the other one. What the four episodes say about a partnership seen from inside.

October 16, 20265 min read1,244 words

David Duchovny directed three episodes of The X-Files and Gillian Anderson directed one, and all four are built by removing the other lead. Duchovny's The Unnatural confines Scully to two scenes and spends its middle in 1947, where neither agent exists. His Hollywood A.D. replaces the partnership with a parody of it, played by other actors. His William, in the ninth season, has Mulder in it only as a DNA result. Anderson's all things puts Mulder on a plane to England in the first act and leaves Scully alone in Washington.

Two actors who had spent six or seven years in a two-shot each used the chair to find out what the show was when the two-shot was gone, and they came back with different answers.

Duchovny's three

Duchovny told Cinefantastique that he directed The Unnatural, which aired April 25, 1999, partly to protect his script. "I guess I've been disappointed in the show's execution," he said of material he had a hand in; he had shared story credit with Carter on Colony, Anasazi and Talitha Cumi. This time he wanted his own vision carried through, and he called directing "a part of the writing process."

The vision is a period fable. Arthur Dales tells Mulder about Josh Exley, a Black ballplayer for the Roswell Grays in 1947 who is secretly an alien, and the hour lives in the flashbacks. Darren McGavin, who had played Dales, fell ill days into the shoot, so M. Emmet Walsh stepped in as the character's brother and Fredric Lehne played the younger Dales. Jesse L. Martin carries the episode as Exley; Duchovny found during production that Martin "turned out to have a beautiful singing voice" and wrote a scene to use it. Bill Roe's warm photography, the show's palette turned toward amber, earned the series its 1999 Emmy nomination for cinematography.

Scully gets an opening argument over a fat-free tofutti cone and a closing batting lesson at night. Duchovny gave her the last line, "Shut up, I'm playing baseball," and said what Mulder was supposed to have learned was "the value of just loving the game." The partner becomes a frame, and the emotional center goes to a surrogate, a man who hides what he is in order to be allowed to play. Exley is Mulder with the longing made literal.

Hollywood A.D., which aired a year later on April 30, 2000, removes the partnership by duplicating it. A producer, played by the comic Wayne Federman and named after him, shadows the agents on a church-bombing case and turns it into a film in which Garry Shandling plays Mulder and Téa Leoni, Duchovny's wife at the time, plays Scully. Carter and Minnie Driver sit in the audience at the premiere. The centerpiece is a three-way split screen of Mulder, Scully and Skinner each in a hotel bubble bath, a phone call staged, as Cinefantastique put it, "a la Pillow Talk." The episode's zombies come to life on the soundstage and tango after the real agents have left; the ending, Duchovny said, "happens behind their backs."

He also told the Seattle Times the episode was "probably darker than just about any show we've ever done, if you think about it," and that he wanted "realness and fakeness on all different levels." A strange claim about a comedy with dancing corpses, until you see what he gave Scully: a resurrection story, religious fanatics, and a test of belief she has to pass alone. Even here the partner interests him as a problem of faith, and the real Mulder and Scully spend the climax hiding on a standing set while the plot resolves without them.

William, which aired April 28, 2002, completes the pattern. Duchovny directed it from a story credited to him, Carter and Frank Spotnitz, with Carter writing the teleplay. A disfigured man appears in the X-Files office, Doggett tests his DNA, and the results point toward Mulder, who is otherwise absent. The episode concerns a Mulder who is nowhere, investigated by people who never knew him, and it ends with Scully giving up their son. Duchovny spent his last directing job on the show staging his own disappearance.

all things, April 2000

Anderson's single episode, all things, aired April 9, 2000, three weeks before Hollywood A.D.. It was the first hour of the series directed by a woman and, as far as I can find, the only one a woman both wrote and directed. It behaves like the work of someone who has waited a long time to be allowed to make a decision.

The first decision is the opening. Scully dresses in a dim bedroom while Mulder sleeps a few feet away, an image the show had spent seven years not giving its audience, delivered as a flash-forward before a word of plot. The second is to send Mulder to England to chase crop circles, which the hour treats as a joke, and to let Scully carry the rest. A chain of coincidences leads her to Daniel Waterston, played by Nicolas Surovy, a married doctor she had an affair with in medical school. He collapses; his daughter still hates her; a woman Scully follows down the street leads her into a Buddhist temple, where she has a vision of the life she did not choose.

The technique is unlike anything else in the series. Anderson shoots Scully walking to Moby's "The Sky Is Broken." She drops a stack of papers in slow motion so that a page about the heart chakra can land face up. The camera stays on a curtain cord knocking against a wall. The A.V. Club gave the hour a C and said it "wants to be a poem" and keeps getting dragged back into the formula it is trying to escape. It ends with Scully telling Mulder the whole story and falling asleep while he is still talking about fate.

Anderson puts Scully nowhere but in Scully. There is no surrogate and no parody. She sends Mulder away to make room for silence, and the result is an hour organized by Scully's attention and not by a case; the slow motion, the lingering, the pop song are all ways of filming attention itself.

Manners, in Fangoria

Duchovny tested whether the show's ideas could survive being handed to other people, a ballplayer, a comedian, a stranger in the basement office. Anderson tested whether one of its two people could hold the screen without the argument that had defined her. Neither test flattered the format. The series had sold itself for years on the two-shot, on the belief that the show was the conversation between these two people, and its own leads, given the camera, each suggested otherwise: that Mulder was a set of ideas that could be reassigned, and that Scully was a consciousness that did not need him in the room.

The partnership survived, and the show ran two more seasons. Kim Manners, who directed more episodes of the series than anyone, had once thought actors should compete for directing jobs like everyone else, since a slot "does take food out of another director's mouth." By 2000 he had changed his mind, and he told Fangoria why. "They have given so much of their own personal lives toward the success of this show that they've earned the right to direct," he said. Their lives centered on the series, and so "the only thing fresh for them to do is direct."
