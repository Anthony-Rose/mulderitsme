After the Basement: The Lesson Was the Failure

The X-Files is credited with the procedural-serial hybrid and a writers' room that produced half the showrunners of the 2000s. Its most powerful legacy was neither. It was the example of a great show that could not end, and the generation that built its own series to avoid becoming it.

October 16, 20266 min read1,476 words

Legacies are usually counted in imitations, and The X-Files has plenty: the two-track structure of weekly cases over a long arc, the dark frame that network drama eventually adopted as a default, the alumni list. All of that is real. But the show's deepest influence on the television that followed was not something it did well. It was something it did badly and publicly, for years, in front of the people who would go on to run the medium. The X-Files proved that a serialized mystery on American television could become the most talked-about drama in the country and still be unable to pay off its own story. The shows that learned most from it learned by refusing to be it.

That is an uncomfortable thesis for a site devoted to the series, so it is worth stating plainly what it does not mean. It does not mean the mythology was a cheat from the start, or that the show was a failure. It means that the most consequential thing the series taught its successors was a negative lesson about endings, and that the proof is in what those successors said and built.

The cautionary tale, on the record

In January 2007, at the Television Critics Association press tour in Pasadena, the producers of Lost were asked about their show's future. Carlton Cuse reached for The X-Files. "That was a great show that probably ran two seasons too long," he said, and then: "That is a cautionary tale for us." He and Damon Lindelof said they had begun talking to ABC about picking an end point. The network, which at first said no such talks had taken place, agreed that May to a 2010 finish, and Lost ended with its date fixed three seasons in advance.

It is hard to overstate how unusual that was. The commercial logic of American network television had always been that a hit runs until it stops being one. Lost argued for the right to stop while it was still a hit, and the argument it made was The X-Files. A show that had reinvented the serialized mystery was now the example its heirs cited when asking to be allowed to end. Whatever one thinks of how Lost used its negotiated ending, the negotiation itself was a structural change in the medium, and the series under discussion was Carter's.

Gilligan's refusal

The second proof is Breaking Bad, and it is more interesting because its creator came from inside the room. Vince Gilligan wrote some of The X-Files' best standalone hours, Pusher, Bad Blood, Drive, and he has told the Television Academy that it was Drive, in 1998, where he first worked with Bryan Cranston and realized the actor could play what he called a sympathetic creep. A decade later he built Walter White on that discovery.

The usual way to tell this is as a casting anecdote. The better way is to notice what Gilligan took from the show and what he left behind. Breaking Bad has no mythology. It has no hidden conspiracy that advances in a few episodes a season and defers its answers to May. It is one story, about one man, and its creator has described it as close-ended by design. Everything Gilligan absorbed from The X-Files was the standalone's discipline: a contained situation, a guest actor given room to be human, a moral pressure that resolves inside the hour. Everything he declined was the long game. The most acclaimed drama of the following decade was made by an X-Files writer who kept the show's episodes and rejected its architecture.

The alumni, and what they ran

The writers' room did become a kind of academy, and the record is remarkable. Howard Gordon went on to 24 and co-created Homeland, winning the Emmy for its pilot script with Alex Gansa and Gideon Raff. Frank Spotnitz, who wrote more episodes than anyone but Carter, brought The Man in the High Castle to Amazon after other outlets passed. Glen Morgan and James Wong rewrote a spec script that Jeffrey Reddick had written as an X-Files episode called "Flight 180" and never submitted; Wong directed it as Final Destination in 2000 and it became a franchise. David Nutter, a mainstay of the early seasons from Ice to Clyde Bruckman's Final Repose, became the industry's pilot specialist, with twenty-one of twenty-four pilots picked up to series by one count, among them Smallville, Supernatural, Arrow and The Flash.

Look at that list as a set of structural choices rather than a roll of honor. 24 is built on a clock, a ticking constraint that forces resolution by design. Homeland reinvents itself season to season. The Man in the High Castle is a closed alternate world with a novel's shape. Final Destination is a rules machine in which the only mystery is who dies next. None of the alumni, with one exception, built a show on an open-ended conspiracy that promises a truth and keeps the promise in escrow. They had seen how that ends.

The exception

The exception is Supernatural, and it proves the rule by paying the full price. Kim Manners, who directed more X-Files episodes than anyone, joined it in its first season as a director and executive producer and worked on it until his death in January 2009. The series copied the model more faithfully than any other: two investigators in a car, a monster a week, a demonic mythology running underneath. It premiered in 2005 and ended in November 2020 after fifteen seasons, by which point it had staged an apocalypse, survived it, and kept going for a decade. Its finale divided a fan base that had stayed through years of diminishing returns, and the loudest response was anger. Anyone who watched the last seasons of The X-Files knew the feeling. The one descendant that adopted the engine whole inherited the exhaust.

Carter's own experiments

Carter himself tested the lesson before anyone else did. Millennium, which premiered on Fox on October 25, 1996, gave Lance Henriksen's Frank Black a darker world than Mulder's and a mythology, the Millennium Group, that the show rebuilt from the ground up when Glen Morgan and James Wong took over its second season, and then rebuilt again in its third. It lasted three seasons. The Lone Gunmen, the spinoff about Mulder's hacker allies, premiered on March 4, 2001 with a pilot, credited to Carter, Spotnitz, Gilligan and John Shiban, in which conspirators try to remotely fly a passenger jet into the World Trade Center; the plane pulls up at the last second. Six months later the episode became the most unsettling footnote in the franchise, and Spotnitz told TV Guide the following summer that almost no one had noticed the connection. The show had already been cancelled after thirteen episodes.

Neither spinoff ran long enough to face the problem the parent show faced. But both show Carter reaching for the same device, a secret organization whose meaning is deferred, and both suggest he understood its appeal better than its cost. When Fox brought The X-Files back in 2016 and 2018, the revival reopened the conspiracy twice more and closed it twice more. A new series from Ryan Coogler, ordered by Hulu this week and discussed elsewhere on this site, is reported to start from new agents rather than Mulder and Scully. If that holds, it is the franchise itself finally drawing the conclusion its imitators drew in 2007.

What the positive legacies actually were

None of this erases what the show gave the medium on purpose. The hybrid of case and arc is now the default shape of genre television, and it was The X-Files that proved a network audience would follow both tracks at once. The 35mm darkness, examined in this site's first essay, became the look of serious drama once the screens caught up. The guest-star economy, in which a Bryan Cranston or a Peter Boyle, who won an Emmy for Clyde Bruckman's Final Repose, could be handed an hour and trusted to carry it, is where a generation of character actors and the writers who loved them learned their trade.

But a legacy is measured by what people do with it, and what the people who came out of that room did was build shows that could end. They kept the episode and dropped the escrow. They put clocks on their stories and dates on their finales. They cited the show by name when asking their networks for the right to stop. The X-Files changed television less by what it showed could be done than by what it showed could not, and the proof of its influence is that no major drama since has been allowed to promise the truth for nine years without being asked, by its own makers, when it intends to deliver.
