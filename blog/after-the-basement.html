After the Basement: How The X-Files Rewired Television

A procedural with a serialized conspiracy underneath, a writers' room that became a showrunner academy, and a look that prestige TV absorbed. Tracing what the show left behind.

October 16, 20263 min read638 words

It's tempting to measure a show's influence by its imitators, and The X-Files had plenty. But its deeper legacy is structural. It changed how a television series could be built, it trained a generation of people who went on to run their own, and it pushed the visual standards of network drama toward cinema.

Two shows in one

The X-Files ran on two tracks. Most weeks it was a procedural: a self-contained case, solved or abandoned by the end of the hour. Running underneath was a serialized conspiracy, the "mythology," which advanced in a smaller number of episodes and carried over across seasons.

That hybrid was not entirely new, but the show made it a model. It let casual viewers drop in on any week while giving dedicated fans a long story to follow and argue about. Many of the genre series that followed, from Fringe to Supernatural, use the same structure: weekly cases laid over a long arc.

The hybrid also has costs, and The X-Files demonstrated those too. A mythology that runs for years has to keep expanding without resolving, and the show's later conspiracy episodes struggled under that weight. Series that came after learned from both its success and its fatigue.

A school for showrunners

The show's writers' room became one of the most important training grounds in modern television. Vince Gilligan, who wrote episodes like Pusher, Bad Blood and Drive, went on to create Breaking Bad. He cast Bryan Cranston in that series because of Cranston's performance in Drive, and the network was reportedly persuaded after seeing the episode.

Howard Gordon went on to run 24 and co-develop Homeland. Frank Spotnitz developed The Man in the High Castle. Glen Morgan and James Wong moved into features, writing the first Final Destination film, which Wong directed. On the directing side, David Nutter became one of the most in-demand pilot directors in television.

That's an extraordinary record for a single show, and it suggests that what the X-Files taught wasn't a formula but a way of working: genre stories told with ambition, specificity and an eye for character.

Carter's other worlds

Chris Carter extended the X-Files sensibility himself. Millennium, which premiered in October 1996, followed a former FBI profiler with a disturbing ability to see through killers' eyes. It was darker and more apocalyptic than its parent show and found a devoted following of its own. The Lone Gunmen, a spinoff about Mulder's hacker allies, premiered on March 4, 2001 with a pilot in which rogue officials plot to fly a hijacked airliner into the World Trade Center. Six months later that plot became one of the most unsettling coincidences in television history.

The look

The show's darkness has also become television's default for serious drama. Today, a prestige series that lets faces fall into shadow and trusts the audience to read a dim frame is unremarkable. In 1993, on network television, it was a provocation. The X-Files did it for nine seasons, on 35mm, at a weekly pace, and proved that viewers would follow.

Descendants

You can see the show's fingerprints on Stranger Things, which premiered in 2016 with its secret government lab, small-town disappearances and flashlights in the woods. You can see them in true-crime podcasts and in the whole culture of online investigation, where amateurs gather evidence and argue about what it means. The idea of the obsessed, slightly isolated investigator chasing something the authorities dismiss has become one of the defining figures of contemporary storytelling.

What lasts

Influence fades as it spreads, until a show's innovations become simply the way things are done. That's the clearest sign of how much The X-Files changed. Watching it now, much of what was radical about it looks normal, because so much of television has followed it into the dark.
