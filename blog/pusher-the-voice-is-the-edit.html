The Voice Is the Edit

Robert Modell has no makeup, no effect and no dark to hide in. His power is a man talking, filmed as ordinary coverage, which puts the viewer in the same chair as his victims. Pusher is the hour where The X-Files came closest to admitting what a cut does.

November 5, 20267 min read1,509 words

A man in a grocery store is surrounded by armed agents and says, "Let's get this show on the road." In the car he talks to the officer at the wheel about a color, cerulean blue, and the car drifts into traffic and under a truck whose side reads Cerulean Hauling. By the title card the audience knows the whole method and the agents know none of it. Zack Handlen, at The A.V. Club, called the villain "a very human monster who manages to be both well-drawn and unsympathetic," and the word to hold onto is human. Robert Patrick Modell has no stretch, no liver-colored skin, no ink in his eyes. He is a man in ordinary clothes who talks.

That is the problem Vince Gilligan handed Rob Bowman early in 1996. The show's grammar was built around a thing the camera declined to show: the flashlight found a shape and the cut arrived before the shape resolved. Here there is nothing to withhold. Modell's power is speech, and speech cannot be kept at the edge of a frame. So the episode films persuasion the way television films any conversation, a face and then the other face, and lets the viewer hear every word of the suggestion, uncut, exactly as the victim hears it. The viewer sits where the victim sits.

Where the voice came from

In a Fox online chat in 2000 he said the teaser came first: "I stole the idea for the teaser from the ending of an old movie script," one he had written the previous summer, in which the hero uses post-hypnotic suggestion on the villain. He never managed to fit that scene into the film, so he spent it here, with mind control added as the supernatural twist. Chris Carter had solicited Soft Light from him as a freelancer the season before and told Cinefantastique that when Gilligan's agent called the following season to say he was ready for a staff job, "I scooped him right up." Pusher was his first script on staff.

He gave the villain three names on purpose. In a 2026 interview with Polygon, excerpted at the fan archive EatTheCorn, he said the middle name is his brother's and the surname just sounded right, but the rule was firm: "a bad guy has got to have three names, like Lee Harvey Oswald." He also offered a translation for the present: "Thirty years later, it'd be called 'Influencer' instead of 'Pusher.'"

Casting a voice

The casting history shows how exposed the design was. Gilligan told Sci-Fi TV in 1998 that Carter had been interested in Lance Henriksen for Modell, before Millennium. The actor then cast in Los Angeles read well and withdrew at the eleventh hour for a television movie, and Robert Wisden, a Vancouver actor, was hired at the last minute. "We were going out on a limb, and it could have been an epic nightmare," Gilligan said. In this hour the guest star is the effect. Wisden got it. Handlen credits him with "a wonderful snarky contempt"; Cinefantastique's third-season guide, which put the hour among the series' best, found that Wisden "makes you feel a bit sorry for him." Within five years he was a Gemini nominee for The Sheldon Kennedy Story.

What Wisden plays is calm, and the calm is the only evidence the show offers that anything paranormal is happening. Scully reads a brain tumor as the whole explanation, and the episode never contradicts her with a picture.

Persuasion in coverage

Walk through the pushes and count the effects. Frank Burst, the agent who brought the case to Mulder and Scully, dies of a heart attack on a telephone while Modell talks to him about his cholesterol and Mulder shouts at him to hang up. Modell fixes his attention on a judge and the verdict comes back not guilty. He walks into the Bureau's records room, and Holly, a records technician, pepper-sprays Skinner and beats him on the floor. At a driving range an agent named Collins sets himself on fire. A phone call, a courtroom, an office, a parking lot. The only images Bowman adds are the ones the victims produce. The cause is a sentence, which a camera cannot photograph.

Mulder fights back with the same instrument. He tells Modell his shoelace is untied, waits for the glance down, and says, "Made you look." Cinefantastique's reviewer observed that the taunt is what turns Modell's interest onto Mulder and leads, two acts later, to the ending. The hero's one move is to steer the villain's eye with a phrase, which is what the villain does to everyone else and what an editor does to you. A cut is an instruction about where to look and when. Modell gives the instruction out loud.

The one big shot

The showiest shot Gilligan remembers is a picture of a consequence. He told Polygon he asked how the shot of the truck hitting the camera was going to be done, "because you want it to be really badass," and was told there was a way: a mirror on a wooden frame in the middle of the road, at 45 degrees to both camera and truck, the camera 30 feet off to the side, and a stunt driver who put a semi straight through the glass. The crash is the end of a sentence about a color. The hour's spectacle went to the full stop.

Close-ups and a fire alarm

The climax, in a hospital, is where the method pays. Mulder goes in without his gun, leaving it with Scully. Modell takes him, and what follows is Russian roulette: Mulder puts a revolver to his own head and clicks on an empty chamber, then turns it on an unarmed Scully. The Cinefantastique guide described the construction. The sequence "consists for the most part of a series of close-ups," and the struggle is so legible in the faces that "the scene could have been done without even its sparse dialogue." Bowman has three faces and a gun, and the suggestion lives in the looks.

Scully ends it with a noise. She pulls the fire alarm and Mulder shoots Modell. A voice is beaten by a louder sound rather than a picture. Modell ends in a coma, and Scully takes Mulder's hand, a gesture Meghan Deans at Reactor read as a hint that he should let this one go. The Television Academy's index of Gilligan's 2011 oral history files the episode under his dealings with Standards and Practices and notes a limit on showing a gun held to a head during a game of Russian roulette.

The sequel that added pictures

"I'm really proud of Pusher," Gilligan told Sci-Fi TV; "Rob Bowman really nailed it." Then, with Tim Minear, he wrote Kitsunegari, directed by Daniel Sackheim and aired January 4, 1998, and the sequel reveals the original's design by abandoning it. Modell escapes. A prosecutor turns up dead, covered in blue paint. A Japanese ideogram meaning foxhunt is on the walls. A second pusher, Modell's sister, is behind it, and Modell himself, in Gilligan's words, "is actually a good guy in this episode." The writers "TRIED to throw viewers a curve ball," he said, and it was not well received; the experience left him "a little gunshy about sequels." Handlen gave it a C+ and called it a "disappointingly apt example of how sequels often fail."

The reason sits in the paint. Pusher had a villain who worked in words and left nothing behind but the damage. Kitsunegari gives the power a signature, a color on a corpse and calligraphy on a wall, a clue to be photographed. Once the push can be seen it is just another X-File, and the sequel's Modell, who kills nobody and dies when his sister pushes his heart to a stop, has lost what made him frightening: a plain voice and no evidence.

The camera as pusher

Deans drew the line from Modell to Walter White. Asked about it, Gilligan said "I guess there are similarities" and that he is the last to notice such things. The resemblance is less the tumor than the method. His long project has been the ordinary man who makes other people act by talking, and this hour was the first time he found that one actor in close-up could carry it with no help from the effects department. The X-Files had spent three seasons refusing to show its monsters. In Pusher it had one that could not be shown, and a man telling you where to look turned out to be all it needed.

In the office on the Monday morning after the broadcast, Gilligan told Carter, as he remembers it, that this was the best he could do. Carter, by Gilligan's account, looked at him seriously and said quietly that he shouldn't say such things, "because this job, for all of us, is about getting better with every script." Gilligan was still quoting the line thirty years later. It worked on him.
