Visual grammar

How the show saw

Network television in 1993 was mostly bright, flat and evenly lit so it would read on a small screen. The X-Files went the other way, and most of what people mean by its look comes down to six habits.

DARKNESS

Black as a working color

Large parts of the frame are allowed to fall off into true black. The audience fills that space with dread, and the budget never has to show a monster it can't afford.

John S. Bartley, who photographed the first three seasons, shot at T2.8 even in daylight interiors and pushed Kodak's EXR stocks rather than add lamps; he said the stock let him go as much as five stops under, and editors sometimes told him they could not see the scene on their monitors. A still photographer summed up the method to him: "You light the walls, but you don't light the actors." Doorways, corridors and the far wall were lit. People found their light or stayed a shape. The Television Academy gave Bartley the Emmy in 1996 for Grotesque, an hour that is almost nothing but flashlights moving through a studio full of gargoyles.

THE BEAM

The flashlight as a second camera

Handheld flashlights are the key light in hundreds of scenes. Wherever an agent points the beam is wherever the audience gets to look, which makes the lighting plan and the point of view one and the same.

The actors carried xenon flashlights, and the gaffers kicked the beams back with Rosco pebble bounce so an agent ended up lit by her own torch. In Squeeze and Tooms the ducts and crawlspaces are searched by flashlight, and the horror is how small the cone is against how much duct there is. Darkness Falls makes the rule the plot: the thing in the woods moves only in the dark, and the characters live as long as the generator does. The device outlasted three cinematographers because it was never a lighting style. It was a rule about what the fiction was allowed to know, which is the argument of The Light in the Character's Hand. The exception proves it: Pusher has a villain whose power is speech and nowhere to point a beam, so Rob Bowman shot it as plain coverage, as The Voice Is the Edit describes.

ATMOSPHERE

Haze and hard backlight

Smoke in the air turns every light source into a visible shaft. Figures are often rimmed from behind and left unlit from the front, reading as silhouettes before they read as people.

Bartley's crews pumped steam into exteriors so that a single street lamp became a column of light, and a stranger in a doorway, rimmed from behind with nothing on the face, became the show's standard image of a threat. The idiom was not new; smoke, shafts and rim light were already the house style of 1980s advertising and the Scott brothers' features. What the show added was restraint about the face. In a Scott frame the backlight flatters. Here it withholds, and a person is only identified when a flashlight reaches them.

PLACE

Weather as production design

The Pacific Northwest supplied overcast skies, wet asphalt and forests that swallow light. The flat gray daylight became as recognizable as the night work.

For five seasons the weather was a department: matched gray daylight, rain that turned a night street into reflections, real mist for free. In 1998 production moved to Los Angeles, and the mist that had come free in Vancouver now had to be made on purpose. Bill Roe answered with hard sun: Drive, almost entirely daylight inside a moving car, won him the ASC award in 1999, and Agua Mala, a hurricane built from nothing in a dry city, won him another in 2000. The look stopped being a gift of climate and became a set of decisions, which is the case made in Los Angeles Made the Look a Choice.

THE INSERT

Evidence in close-up

Slides, implants, bite marks, dental x-rays. The show cuts to objects the way a procedural cuts to clues, and the inserts carry a lot of the storytelling that dialogue doesn't.

The inserts are where Scully's half of the argument lives. An elongated fingerprint on an air vent in Squeeze, a worm moving under skin in Ice, a metal chip lifted from the back of her own neck in The Blessing Way: each is a thing a camera can photograph, held still, in focus, in a frame otherwise built on what cannot be seen. The autopsy bay was her flashlight. The teaser worked the same way at the scale of the hour, putting the body in front of the audience before either agent had heard of the case, so that every insert afterward was the agents catching up; Three Minutes Ahead of the Badge traces that habit.

OFF-SCREEN

Sound beyond the frame

Mark Snow's synthesized drones and the sound design work in the space the image refuses to show. A low tone tells you something is in the room before the camera does.

Snow wrote between thirty and thirty-five minutes of score for every forty-five minute episode, alone on a Synclavier in Santa Monica, in five or six days, to a brief from Carter that ruled out anything melodic or traditional. The result was closer to sound design than scoring: pads built on tritones and spaced major seconds, intervals that refuse to resolve, shifting a half step before the picture gives a reason. A synthesizer has no address; it seems to come from the walls. On a show about things that might be in the room, that was the correct instrument, and the two orchestral film scores sound less like the series than any episode. The whistled theme everyone can hum is the least representative thing he wrote for it. Music Without an Address makes the full case.

Where they went

The frame changed first. The series was framed for 4:3 and protected for 16:9, and the 2015 remaster now streaming everywhere opens the sides and trims the top, which is where the show kept its doorways and its headroom; The Frame Had a Ceiling takes the measurements. The darkness traveled furthest. Drama adopted the fallen-off black as the look of seriousness, and by April 2019 a Game of Thrones battle episode could be criticized mainly for being too dark to see. Supernatural took the flashlights, the car and the Vancouver rain, with Manners producing. Fringe took the two-track structure and moved to Vancouver for its second season. Stranger Things put children with flashlights in the woods. The habit least imitated is the one that mattered most: the dark shows that followed are dark because it reads as grave, not because a character is carrying the only light. Ryan Coogler's Hulu pilot, photographed by Autumn Durald Arkapaw, is the next test. On Sinners she lit whole ceilings so an unbroken camera could turn in any direction and nothing could be withheld. That is Bartley's method run backward.

The truth is out there.
